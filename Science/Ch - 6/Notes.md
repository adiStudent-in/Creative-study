## 1. The Concept of Force (6.1)

- **What a force can do:**
  - Make an object **move from rest** — e.g., kicking a football (Fig. 6.1a).
  - **Change speed and direction** of a moving object — e.g., a cricket bat striking a ball (Fig. 6.1b).
  - **Change shape** of an object — e.g., fingers squeezing a lemon (Fig. 6.1c).
- **Force needs direction + magnitude + unit:**
  - Just like position, displacement, velocity and acceleration (Chapter 4), force is a quantity for which **direction must be specified along with magnitude and unit**.
  - Examples: friction acts **opposite to motion**; like magnetic poles repel / unlike poles attract; like charges repel / unlike charges attract (electrostatic force); Earth attracts objects **towards itself** (gravitational force); buoyant force acts **upward** on an object in liquid.
  - If **magnitude or direction or both** change, the effect of the force changes.
- **SI unit:** **newton** (written with small 'n'), symbol **N**. Magnitude expresses strength.
  - When a unit is named after a person, full name is small case (newton, not Newton) but symbol is capitalised (N, not n).

### 1.1 Measuring Force — Spring Balance

- A **spring balance** measures weight and, in general, magnitude of force.
- **Weight** = gravitational force with which Earth pulls the object.
- Pulling the free end measures the force with which you pull the spring inside.
- **How much is 1 N?** Holding a **100 g mass** in the palm — upward force by palm ≈ **1 N**.
- **Scale of forces:**
  - Smallest directly felt in everyday life ≈ **millinewtons** ($$10^{-3} \text{ N}$$), e.g., a light touch.
  - Scientists can measure down to **yoctonewtons** ($$10^{-24} \text{ N}$$) in specialised experiments (as of 2026).

## 2. Balanced and Unbalanced Forces (6.2)

- Real life: **more than one force** usually acts on an object.
  - Pushing a box: your push forward + **friction opposite to motion** (Fig. 6.3a).
  - Ball floating on water: **gravitational force downwards** + **buoyant force upwards** (Fig. 6.3b).

| Type | Meaning | Motion |
|---|---|---|
| **Balanced forces** | Equal in magnitude, opposite in direction; net force = 0 | Rope does not move; rest stays rest, uniform motion continues |
| **Unbalanced forces** | Magnitudes unequal or directions not cancelling; net force ≠ 0 | Motion changes; moves in direction of larger/net force |

- **Tug of war (Fig. 6.4):**
  - Both teams pull equal opposite → rope does not move → balanced.
  - One team pulls harder (larger magnitude) → rope moves toward larger force → unbalanced.
- **Opposite directions, unequal magnitudes (Fig. 6.4b):**
$$\text{magnitude of net force} = \text{difference of magnitudes}$$
  - Direction = along the larger force.
- **Same direction (Fig. 6.5)** — e.g., two people pushing a stalled car:
$$\text{magnitude of net force} = \text{sum of magnitudes}$$
  - Direction = same as the two forces.
- **Beyond scope:** forces at an angle to each other, and equal-opposite forces on two ends of an extended object causing **rotation** (e.g., handlebar, tap — Fig. 6.7) — you will learn these in higher grades.

> ⚠️ **Important Rule:** Multiple forces may act on an object, but its motion depends **only on the net force**.

**Worked Example (Example 6.1 — 10 N and 6 N on a block, Fig. 6.6):**
- (a) Both to the right: Net = $$10 \text{ N} + 6 \text{ N} = 16 \text{ N}$$ towards the right.
- (b) 10 N right, 6 N left: Net = $$10 \text{ N} - 6 \text{ N} = 4 \text{ N}$$ towards the right.
- (c) 10 N left, 6 N right: Net = $$10 \text{ N} - 6 \text{ N} = 4 \text{ N}$$ towards the left.

## 3. The Force of Friction: Often Overlooked but Always Present (6.3)

- Box at rest on floor, you push forward (Fig. 6.10): it may not move until you push harder because **friction between box bottom and floor opposes your push**.
- Box moves only when applied force magnitude **> friction magnitude**, so net force acts in direction of motion.
- **Other forces on a pushed object (Fig. 6.11):**
  - **Gravitational force (weight)** downwards.
  - **Normal force** by surface, upward, perpendicular to surface. These two are **balanced**.
  - Applied force forward, friction backward (horizontal pair).
  - Air also exerts friction, but usually so small it can be **neglected**.
- If you stop pushing a moving box / stop pedalling a bicycle / stop pushing a ball, it slows and stops — **not** because motion needs continuous force, but because **unopposed friction** brings it to rest. To keep it moving you must continuously apply force **to counter friction**.

### 3.1 Activity 6.1 — Different Surfaces, Different Friction

- Stack 4 ₹10 coins taped together; rubber band stretched between finger-thumb at marks A, B, stretched back to C; release stack from C.
- After losing contact with rubber band, velocity decreases gradually; measure distance from C. Repeat twice.
- Repeat on wooden table top → laminated table top → polished marble/tile floor (same A, B, C distances).
- Observation: smoother surface → **travels larger distance**, velocity decreases **more slowly**.
- Before release: stationary → forces balanced. On release: rubber-band force forward > friction → net forward → velocity 0 → some value (acceleration forward, Fig. 6.13a). After losing contact: only **friction opposite motion** (Fig. 6.13b) → slows to rest.

### 3.2 Activity 6.2 — Measuring Friction with a Spring Balance

- Spring balance horizontal (zero checked), wooden block attached to hook (Fig. 6.14). Pull with gradually increasing force; note reading **when block just starts moving**.
- At neither increasing nor decreasing velocity, net force = 0, so spring reading ≈ **magnitude of friction**.
- Repeat on all four surfaces. Compare: smallest reading where coins travelled largest distance; largest reading where distance was smallest.
- Conclusion: **smaller friction → velocity decreases more slowly → larger distance before rest.**

### 3.3 Thought Experiment → Galileo

- Imagine object + horizontal floor so smooth that **friction = 0**. Repeat Activity 6.1: will velocity decrease? Will it ever stop, or continue forever?
- **Meet a Scientist — Galileo Galilei (17th century):** For ages it was mistakenly thought a force was needed to maintain constant-velocity motion. Galileo argued through thought experiments: if a body moves along a horizontal plane and **all impediments removed, it will continue indefinitely**.
- **Meet a Scientist — Isaac Newton:** used word **'inertia'** for tendency to resist change in rest or uniform motion; framed **first law**; presented **three laws in 1687** — defining moment in history of science. Unit of force named after him.
- **Key inference:**
  - Net force needed to **set at-rest object moving**.
  - Once moving (friction zero), **no further force needed** to keep constant velocity.
  - Force needed to **change velocity** (magnitude or direction) or stop.

## 4. Newton's First Law of Motion (6.4)

> **Statement:** An object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues to move with a constant velocity, unless a net force acts upon the object.

- If net force = 0 → cannot begin to move or change velocity → **acceleration = 0**.
- At rest = zero velocity. Constant velocity = no change in **magnitude or direction**; if non-zero, motion is in a **straight line, same direction, same speed**.

**Worked Example (Example 6.2):**
Person pushes moving box forward with force **equal** to friction backward → equal-opposite → balanced → net = 0 → box **continues with constant velocity** (does not come to rest).

**Worked Example (Example 6.3 — Graphs with no net force):**
- Possibilities: at rest OR moving with constant velocity.
- At rest: position-time = horizontal line (Fig. 6.15a); velocity-time = zero line (Fig. 6.15b).
- Constant velocity: position-time = straight sloped line (Fig. 6.16a); velocity-time = horizontal non-zero line (Fig. 6.16b).

## 5. Newton's Second Law of Motion (6.5)

- Force produces **acceleration**. Relation between net force and acceleration?
- **Hypothesis 1:** For same object, larger force → larger acceleration. Test by applying different weights (gravitational forces) via cup-pulley-cart.
- **Hypothesis 2:** For same force, smaller mass → larger acceleration. Test by changing cart mass.

### 5.1 Activity 6.3 — Vary Force (Demonstration)

- Cart from cardboard box + 4 ball-bearing wheels + pencil axles (Fig. 6.17a); thread over pipe-pulley to paper cup holding coins/objects (Fig. 6.17b). Cup falls due to gravity, pulling cart with constant force.
- Measure mass of cup + contents. Video cart in slow motion; time $$T_1$$ from start line to pipe over distance $$s$$, $$u = 0$$.
- Double cup mass, repeat → time $$T_2$$.
- Analysis with $$s = \tfrac{1}{2}a_1T_1^2$$ and $$s = \tfrac{1}{2}a_2T_2^2$$ → equating:
$$a_1T_1^2 = a_2T_2^2$$
- Result: increased force (same cart mass) → **increased acceleration**.

### 5.2 Activity 6.4 — Vary Mass (Demonstration)

- Keep cup mass constant (same force); **double cart mass** by adding objects; measure cart mass; repeat timing.
- Result: increased mass (same force) → **decreased acceleration** — acceleration **inversely related** to mass.

> **Statement:** When a net force acts, the object accelerates in the direction of net force. Magnitude of acceleration is **proportional to net force** and **inversely proportional to mass**.

$$a = \dfrac{F}{m} \tag{6.1}$$
$$F = ma \tag{6.2}$$
- Direction of acceleration = direction of net force.
- SI: mass kg, acceleration $$\text{m s}^{-2}$$. If $$m = 1 \text{ kg}$$, $$a = 1 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$:
$$F = (1 \text{ kg})(1 \text{ m s}^{-2}) = 1 \text{ kg m s}^{-2} = 1 \text{ N}$$
> **1 newton** = force producing $$1 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$ on 1 kg mass.

### 5.3 Weight and g

- Freely falling object accelerates with **acceleration due to gravity** $$g$$, unit $$\text{m s}^{-2}$$.
$$F = mg \tag{6.3}$$
- Near Earth's surface $$g = 9.8 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$ (nearly constant); for quick estimates take $$g = 10 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$.
- Note: $$g$$ does **not** depend on mass of object.

> **Threads of Curiosity:** Doubling force should double acceleration (Eq. 6.1) and doubling mass should halve it, but measured change is a little less — apart from measurement errors, **friction at wheels** causes difference.
> **Ready to Go Beyond — Momentum form:** Momentum = mass × velocity (direction same as velocity). Second law: **rate of change of momentum ∝ net force**, in direction of net force. This fuller form works even when mass is not constant.

### 5.4 Applications of Second Law

- **Catching cricket ball (Fig. 6.18):** Fielder pulls hands back → increases time for velocity → 0 → smaller acceleration → smaller force → less injury.
- **Airbags (Fig. 6.19):** On abrupt halt, bag inflates to soft cushion; head/chest push into bag → hitting time increases → smaller acceleration → reduced force (with seat belt, lowers serious injury).
- **Cracking coconut (Fig. 6.20):** Brought down at high velocity to hit hard surface; stops in very short time → ground must exert very large force → shell breaks. Similarly glass packed in bubble wrap/hay increases stopping time.

**Worked Example (Example 6.4 — Weightlifter, Fig. 6.8):**
Bar 10 kg + 10 kg each side → total 30 kg.
$$F = mg = 30 \times 9.8 = 294 \text{ N}$$ downwards.
To keep steady, she applies **294 N upward**.

**Worked Example (Example 6.5 — 25 kg block, max friction 50 N):**
- (i) Push 50 N: applied = friction → balanced → net 0 → **remains stationary**, displacement 0.
- (ii) Push 55 N: Net = $$55 - 50 = 5 \text{ N}$$.
$$a = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{5}{25} = 0.2 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$
$$s = ut + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2 = 0 \times 2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(0.2)(2)^2 = 0.4 \text{ m}$$ forward.

**Worked Example (Example 6.6 — 1500 kg sports car, Fig. 6.21 v-t graph):**
- (i) 0–5 s: straight inclined, $$u = 0$$, $$v = 10 \text{ m s}^{-1}$$, $$t = 5 \text{ s}$$. $$v = u + at$$ → $$10 = 0 + a(5)$$ → $$a = 2 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$. $$F = 1500 \times 2 = 3000 \text{ N}$$ east.
- (ii) 5–10 s: line parallel to time axis → constant velocity → **no force**.
- (iii) 10–15 s: $$u = 10$$, $$v = 0$$, $$t = 5$$ → $$0 = 10 + a(5)$$ → $$a = -2 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$. $$F = 1500 \times (-2) = -3000 \text{ N}$$, negative = opposite motion, i.e., **west**.

## 6. Newton's Third Law of Motion (6.6)

- Force needs **at least two interacting objects**. Kicking a ball (Fig. 6.22): foot pushes ball, ball pushes foot.
- **Activity 6.5:** Sit on wheeled chair, legs up. Push heavy table forward (Fig. 6.23a) → you + chair move **opposite**. Pull table (Fig. 6.23b) → move other way. Conclusion: table pushes you opposite each time.
- **Bicycle without pedalling (Fig. 6.24):** push ground backward with feet → ground pushes feet forward → you + bicycle move forward.
- **Walking/running (Fig. 6.25):** foot pushes ground backward; ground's friction pushes foot **forward** — here friction **helps** motion. Without friction foot would slip back, you would fall. Grooves on soles and tyre treads **increase friction**; hence difficult to walk on wet polished floor/ice, risky to drive on water/snow-covered roads.
- **Bridging Science and Society:** same friction logic explains footwear and tyre design.
- **Activity 6.6 — Equal magnitudes:** Two identical spring balances hooked together, pulled opposite (Fig. 6.26, one end fixed). Readings **same every time**, varied force → opposite forces equal in magnitude.

> **Statement:** Whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first.
> The pair acts on **two different objects**.

- **Climbing coconut/palm tree (Fig. 6.27):** legs push trunk down; friction trunk-on-legs pushes person up equally. Harder on smooth trunks (less friction).
- **Canoe/boat (Fig. 6.28 — answers Think It Over, p.94):** paddle pushes water backward; water pushes paddle + canoe forward equally. Forces on **different objects** (paddle vs water) so do not cancel. Pushing harder → larger forward force → faster. Same paddle force on empty vs passenger-loaded canoe: empty (smaller mass) moves faster — see second law. Other factors: drag, currents, mass, rowing style.
- **Activity 6.7 — Balloon rocket (Fig. 6.29):** Balloon on straw on taut thread; untie neck → stretched rubber pushes air out; rushing air pushes balloon opposite → balloon moves opposite air jet.
- **Rocket launch (Fig. 6.30):** Engine expels gas downward; gas pushes rocket upward equally. Upward force > weight → net upward → lift-off. If engine in space fires along motion, exhaust pushes opposite → slows down — used by **Vikram lander of Chandrayaan-3** to slow for soft landing near Moon's south pole.
- **Applies to non-contact too (Figs. 6.31–6.33):** two bar magnets, two similarly charged balloons, Earth + fruit — all equal-opposite pairs.

> ⚠️ **Important Rule:** Third-law pair on **two different objects** → do not balance. Equal-opposite on **same object** → balance.

**Worked Example (Example 6.7 — Earth and fruit, Fig. 6.33):**
Equal-opposite gravity, but Earth mass huge vs fruit → $$a = F/m$$ tiny for Earth → unnoticeable; fruit accelerates visibly.

**Worked Example (Example 6.8 — Gun recoil):**
0.1 kg bullet fired from 5 kg gun with 2 N force → recoil on gun also **2 N**.
$$a_{\text{gun}} = \dfrac{2}{5} = 0.4 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$
$$a_{\text{bullet}} = \dfrac{2}{0.1} = 20 \text{ m s}^{-2}$$
Equal forces, unequal accelerations because **masses differ**.

## 7. Forces on a System of Objects (6.7)

- Apply laws to **connected objects** as one system.
- Two boxes masses $$m_1, m_2$$ on frictionless surface, joined by string, pulled by $$F$$ on Box 1 (Fig. 6.34). Box 1 pulls Box 2 via string; Box 2 pulls Box 1 equally opposite — this is **tension** $$T$$.
  - Box 1: $$F$$ right, $$T$$ left. Box 2: $$T$$ right.
- Simpler: treat boxes + string as **single system**. **Internal forces** ($$T$$ on both) need not be considered; only **external** ($$F$$) matters:
$$a = \dfrac{F}{\text{mass of system}} = \dfrac{F}{m_1 + m_2} \tag{6.4}$$
  - System accelerates like single object of mass $$m_1 + m_2$$. Individual analysis (higher grades) gives same result.
- Vertical externals (Fig. 6.35): gravity $$(m_1 + m_2)g$$ down balanced by normal $$(N_1 + N_2)$$ up from ground.

> **Threads of Curiosity:** Walking uses complex arm-leg motions, yet overall motion is studied by treating body as single object. Science simplifies when we look at the whole, not just parts.

## 8. At-a-Glance (from Chapter)

- Friction acts **opposite to motion**.
- **First law:** rest stays rest, motion continues constant velocity, unless net force acts.
- **Second law:** net force → acceleration in its direction; magnitude ∝ net force, ∝ 1/mass.
- **Third law:** every exertion has simultaneous equal-opposite return on the other object.
- Friction is always present where surfaces contact; reduced by lubricants, coatings, texturing, streamlining, magnetic levitation. Newton's laws cover everyday to planetary scales; need modification only near massive objects, near light speed, atomic scales.

### Important Notes to Remember

- Motion depends only on **net force**, not individual forces.
- **Balanced (net 0)** → rest stays rest, moving keeps constant velocity ($$a = 0$$); position-time straight (horizontal if rest), velocity-time horizontal.
- **Unbalanced (net ≠ 0)** → acceleration in net-force direction ($$F = ma$$).
- Smooth surface = smaller friction = slower velocity decrease = larger travel distance.
- **100 g ≈ 1 N** feel; **mN ($$10^{-3}$$ N)** light touch; **yN ($$10^{-24}$$ N)** lab limit (as of 2026).
- **g independent of mass**; use 9.8, or 10 for quick estimates (e.g., 30 kg barbell → 294 N).
- Increase time of velocity change → smaller acceleration/force (catch ball, airbags, mats/sand, bubble wrap); decrease time → huge force (coconut smash, bullet stopping).
- Third-law pair: **equal, opposite, different objects, simultaneous** — contact or non-contact (push, friction, magnets, charges, gravity). Equal forces need not give equal accelerations if masses differ (Earth-fruit, gun-bullet 0.4 vs 20 $$\text{m s}^{-2}$$).
- Internal tension cancels in system analysis; only external $$F$$ gives $$a$$.
- Unit spelling: **newton** (small n), symbol **N** (capital N).
