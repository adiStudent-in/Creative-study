01 From Cell to Organism — Hierarchy and Division of Labour

  • Life begins when a single cell divides several times to give rise to a large number of cells. These form skin (protection), muscles (movement), bones (support), nerves (control and coordination) and all other organs.
  • Hierarchy of organisation in multicellular organisms: Cells of similar type performing similar function → Tissue → More than one type of tissue → Organ → Different organs → Organ system → Organism
  • In unicellular organisms such as amoeba, a single cell performs all functions of life.
  • Tissue: a group of cells (similar in structure) that work together to perform a specific function.
  • Formation of different tissues leads to division of labour, which increases efficiency and enables complex life processes.
  • Examples: in animals, muscle tissue enables movement and nervous tissue carries messages; in plants, xylem transports water and minerals while phloem transports food.

02 Why are Plant and Animal Tissues Different?

  • Most plants are fixed in one place and do not move like animals. They need support to stay firm and upright.
  • Plant cells have a cell wall that provides rigidity and strength.
  • Animals can move (although some, such as sponges, are immobile). Without a rigid cell wall, animal cells can change shape easily. This flexibility helps make bodies suitable for locomotion.
  • Mode of nutrition differs: animals have tissues to digest food from different sources; plants have tissues to utilise solar energy for synthesising food through photosynthesis.
  • Plants and animals have distinct tissues for transporting food and water.
  • Growth patterns vary because tissues responsible for growth differ in structure and function.

03 Tissues for Growth in Plants — Meristematic Tissues

Plants grow in three ways:

  • increase in length (height of stem and depth of roots),
  • increase in girth (thickness of stem), and
  • regrowth after cutting branches or grazing by animals.

This growth requires actively dividing cells that together form meristematic tissue.

3.1 Apical Meristem — How do plants grow in length?

Worked Example

Worked Example (Activity 3.1: Onion root tip experiment):

  1. Take two glass jars filled with water; place one onion bulb in each (Jar A and Jar B).
  2. Observe roots for a few days; measure length on days 1, 2 and 3 and record in Table 3.1.
  3. On day 3, cut root tips of bulb in Jar B by about 1 cm; observe for four more days (days 4–7).

Observation:

  • Roots in Jar A continue to grow in length.
  • Roots in Jar B stop growing after tips are cut.

Conclusion: Roots grow only from their tips. Tips consist of continuously dividing cells. This recalls mitosis observed in onion root tips in Chapter 2. Similarly, shoot tips contain actively dividing cells.

  • Plants have growth zones at tips of roots and shoots called apical meristems, which help plants grow in length.

3.2 Lateral Meristem — How do plants grow in girth?

  • Stems of dicot plants increase in diameter or girth over time.
  • Cut trunk of a tree shows ring-like patterns — annual growth rings.
  • Some rings are wide and some narrow, reflecting favourable or unfavourable growth conditions during a particular year.
  • By counting annual rings, scientists can estimate age of a tree and understand climatic conditions under which it grew.
  • Increase in girth occurs due to actively dividing cells arranged in a ring in the stem. These divide and produce new cells inside and outside in a concentric manner, increasing diameter.
  • This tissue is called lateral meristem.
Worked Example

Worked Example (Teak tree data — Table 3.7): Age 5 yr → DBH 4 cm → 5 rings; 10 yr → 8 cm → 10 rings; 20 yr → 24 cm → 20 rings; 25 yr → 28 cm → 25 rings; 30 yr → 32 cm → 30 rings; 40 yr → 40 cm → 40 rings.

  • Diameter increases with age; number of annual rings = age in years. Tissue responsible: lateral meristem located along circumference of stem.

3.3 Intercalary Meristem — How do plants grow after being cut?

  • If tip of a young stem is cut, stem stops growing in length but new branches arise from nodes.
  • Node: point on stem where branches or leaves arise. Internode: part of stem between two nodes.
  • Intercalary meristem is located at the base of internode or just above the node.
  • When hedge is cut, more branches appear giving bushy appearance. Grass reappears after mowing or grazing because of intercalary meristem at nodes of its stem.

3.4 Summary of Three Meristems

Meristem Location Function
Apical meristem Root and shoot tips Increases length
Lateral meristem Along circumference of stems (ring) Increases girth
Intercalary meristem Base of internode / just above node; base of certain plants such as grasses Helps regenerate after cutting; branching

3.5 Characteristics of Meristematic Cells

  • Cells are small, thin-walled, with large prominent nucleus and dense cytoplasm with many organelles.
  • Vacuoles generally absent; cells tightly packed with little or no intercellular space.
  • These characteristics allow continuous and rapid cell division.

3.6 Differentiation — Meristematic to Permanent

  • Due to continuous division, meristematic tissue adds new cells. Some remain meristematic while others lose ability to divide.
  • Cells that lose ability to divide undergo changes in structure and function and become permanent tissues, specialised for support, transport or storage.
  • This process is called differentiation. Meristematic tissue becomes permanent by differentiation.

04 Permanent Tissues

T.S. of sunflower stem shows different groups of cells, each specialised. Permanent tissues can be simple (one type of cell) or complex (more than one type of cell).

4.1 Protective Tissue — Epidermis

  • Epidermis forms outermost layer of plant body. Tightly packed, single layer of flat and rectangular cells. Protects all parts from mechanical injury, water loss, harmful microorganisms and extreme conditions.
  • Cells covered with waxy layer of cutin called cuticle.
  • In very dry habitats, epidermis may have thick cuticle to reduce water loss by transpiration through stomata. Cuticle also protects against mechanical injury and invasion by parasites.
  • Hair-like projections arise from epidermal cells. In roots, root hairs increase surface area for absorption of water and minerals. In leaves, epidermis contains pores called stomata.
  • Stomata help in gaseous exchange and transpiration — evaporation of water vapours through stomata.
  • Transpiration helps water transportation by creating transpiration pull in xylem. It also helps in elimination of wastes.

4.2 Supporting Tissues — Simple Permanent Tissues

Three types — parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma. Each differs in structure and supporting function.

Tissue Living / Dead Wall Chemistry and Structure Function and Location
Parenchyma Living, thin walls, loosely packed with intercellular spaces Thin walls Mainly stores food; photosynthesis in green parts; in aquatic plants specialised parenchyma forms air spaces which help them float
Collenchyma Living, unevenly thickened corners Pectin deposition (chemical giving flexibility like rubber) Provides support and flexibility, allowing stems and tendrils to bend without breaking
Sclerenchyma Mostly dead, thick walls Lignin deposition, hard and strong (woody structure) Found in stems, leaf veins, hard coverings of seeds and nuts such as coconut husk and walnut shell

4.3 Conducting Tissues — Complex Permanent Tissues

Made of different types of cells working together. Xylem and phloem together are complex permanent tissues.

Xylem — transports water and minerals from roots to other parts; also provides strength:

  • Consists of tracheids, vessels, xylem parenchyma and xylem fibres.
  • Tracheids and vessels are tubular and thick-walled.
  • Xylem parenchyma is the only living component of xylem, while tracheids, vessels and xylem fibres are primarily sclerenchymatous.

Phloem — mostly made of living cells; transports food from leaves to other parts:

  • Consists of sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem parenchyma and phloem fibres.
  • Some cells are long and tubular, joined end to end by perforated walls — sieve tubes that transport food.
  • Companion cells are specialised parenchyma cells; regulate cellular functions of sieve tubes; monitor loading and unloading of sugars.
  • Phloem parenchyma stores food materials, resin, tannins and latex.
  • Phloem fibres are primarily sclerenchymatous and provide strength; support sieve tubes.

4.4 Tissue Systems in Plants

Plant tissues are organised into three tissue systems:

  1. Dermal tissue system: outer covering; protects inner parts and reduces water loss.
  2. Ground tissue system: main body between dermal and conducting tissues; includes parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
  3. Vascular tissue system: conducting tissues — xylem and phloem.

05 Animal Tissues

Like plants, animal cells group and specialise. Each tissue performs a specific function.

5.1 Epithelial Tissues — Structure and Functions

  • Forms outer covering (skin) and lines internal organs such as mouth, lungs, blood vessels and intestine.
  • Composed of closely packed cells with very little space — prevents entry of germs, reduces water loss, helps absorption, secretion and movement of substances.
Function Structure Location in the body
Exchange: rapid diffusion of liquids and gases Single layer of thin, flat cells Lining of blood vessels and lungs
Protection: protects underlying tissues from mechanical injury, friction, entry of microbes Many layers; outer cells flat and tightly packed Skin, mouth and oesophagus
Secretion: production and secretion of mucus, enzymes, hormones, sweat, saliva Cells specialised for producing and releasing substances; may be cuboidal or columnar Salivary glands, sweat glands and stomach lining
Sensory functions: smell, taste, sound and balance Specialised receptor cells with hair-like cilia Nostrils, taste buds and inner ear
Absorption: efficient uptake of nutrients, water, etc. Single layer of tall, pillar-like cells, often with hair-like structure Lining of small intestine

5.2 Connective Tissues — How are various parts connected?

  • Tissue that connects and supports other tissues. Both blood and bones are connective tissues.
  • They differ in composition and consistency — blood is fluid, bone is hard — due to matrix, which is watery, soft and jelly-like in blood but hard, solid and rigid in bones.

Blood and its components (Activity 3.2):

  • Red colour due to haemoglobin, an iron-rich protein in Red Blood Cells (RBCs). RBCs live for about 4 months and are replaced regularly.
  • Components of blood: Plasma (55% of total volume) + Formed elements — RBCs, WBCs, platelets (45% of total volume).

  • Cartilage has soft, jelly-like matrix; provides flexibility and cushioning.
  • Tendons connect muscles to bones; ligaments connect bones to bones and prevent excessive movement.

5.3 Muscular Tissues — Can we control movement?

  • Voluntary movements (running, writing, lifting) are under conscious control, carried out by skeletal muscles attached to skeleton.
  • Involuntary movements (food in intestine, beating of heart) occur automatically without conscious control.
Muscle type Location Cell features Movement
Skeletal muscle Attached to skeleton Bundles of long, cylindrical fibres; unbranched, multinucleate (many nuclei), striated (light and dark bands) Voluntary
Smooth muscle Stomach, intestines and other organs Spindle-shaped, single nucleus, no striations Involuntary; slow continuous movements like digestion
Cardiac muscle Only in heart Cylindrical, branched, single nucleus, faint striations Involuntary; works tirelessly and rhythmically without fatigue

5.4 Nervous Tissue — How does the body sense, communicate and respond?

  • Nervous tissue forms control and coordination network. Brain is control centre — coordinates activities, memory and responses.
  • Muscles cannot function independently; receive instructions from nervous tissue. During exercise brain signals heart to beat faster to meet increased oxygen demand.
  • Cells are neurons or nerve cells, specialised to receive, process and transmit messages.
  • Each neuron has three main parts:
    • Cell body — contains nucleus, controls cell activities;
    • Dendrites — receive signals from other neurons;
    • Axon — long fibre carrying messages from cell, ending at axon terminals that transmit messages to other cells.

06 The Musculoskeletal System

  • Made of bones, muscles, joints, cartilage, tendons and ligaments.
  • Helps stand upright, move, maintain posture and protect delicate organs. Functions under control of nervous system.
  • Muscles pull on bones to produce movement. Attached by tendons. When muscle contracts, tendon transmits force to bone, resulting in movement at joint.
  • Adult human skeleton makes up about 12–15 per cent of body weight (varies with age, gender, body composition).

6.2 Skeletal System

  • Framework of bones providing strength and protecting delicate organs. Includes skull, vertebral column and rib cage.
  • From base of skull extends flexible column — backbone or vertebral column (spine) — made of small bones called vertebrae. Supports body, helps stand upright.
  • Between each vertebra is a cartilage disc acting as cushion, allowing flexibility to bend and twist without injuring internal spinal cord.
  • Ribs: 12 pairs forming rib cage protecting heart and lungs. Attached to spine at back and breast bone (sternum) in front, joined by flexible cartilage. Flexibility allows rib cage to expand and contract during breathing, increasing/decreasing chest space so air moves in/out of lungs. Injury makes breathing painful.
  • Maintain posture, proper nutrition, regular exercise and yoga to keep bones strong, muscles fit, joints flexible.

07 Think as a Scientist — From One Cell to an Organism: Totipotency

  • In 1958, F. C. Steward demonstrated that even single cells from vascular phloem of carrot retain ability to regenerate whole plants. He was the first to do so.
  • Grew phloem cells in nutrient medium containing simple sugars and hormones under appropriate conditions. Cells divided to form mass, gradually divided and differentiated into complete plant.
  • Phloem cells first dedifferentiated (regain ability to divide) to form undifferentiated mass of unspecialised cells. When grown with nutrients and growth chemicals, further divided and redifferentiated to form roots, shoot and complete plant.
  • Thus some mature plant cells can undifferentiate, divide and redifferentiate into new plant under specific conditions — totipotency; such cells are totipotent cells. Similar to zygote dividing and differentiating into entire organism.
  • Steps: cross-section of carrot root → 2-mg fragments cultured in nutrient medium; stirring caused single cells to shear into liquid → single cells free in suspension began to divide → embryonic plant developed → plantlet cultured on agar, later planted in soil → adult plant.

7.1 Bridging Science and Society — Crown Gall Disease

  • In nature plant pathologists observed crown gall disease — tumour-like swellings on stems due to rapid uncontrolled cell division. Caused by bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens.
  • Scientists studied how bacterium transfers genetic material into plant cells. Knowledge used in plant tissue culture and genetic engineering.
  • Today Agrobacterium is used as tool to introduce useful genes for production of valuable phytochemicals, improved crops and disease-resistant varieties.

7.2 🔬 Meet the Scientists

  • B. G. L. Swamy — renowned Indian botanist known for plant morphology and anatomy. Book Hasuru Honnu in Kannada — blend of science, satire and culture; botanical excursion in Western Ghats; treasure of plant descriptions, utilisation, conservation, folklores and myths. Won Kendra Sahitya Akademi Award in 1978.
  • Sipra Guha Mukherjee in collaboration with S. C. Maheshwari — breakthrough in plant tissue culture. Developed complete plant through anther culture using artificial nutrient medium under controlled laboratory conditions. Greatly contributed to crop improvement and modern agriculture.

📚 At a Glance (Summary from the Chapter)

  • Tissues are groups of similar cells working together to perform specific functions.
  • Different tissues coordinate to perform life processes in plants and animals.
  • Plant tissues are broadly meristematic and permanent, depending on ability to divide.
  • Functionally, plant tissues are protecting, supporting and conducting tissues.
  • Permanent tissues may be simple (one type of cell) or complex (more than one type).
  • Simple permanent tissues include parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.
  • Complex permanent tissues include xylem and phloem transporting water and food respectively.
  • Animal tissues are four types: epithelial, connective, muscular and nervous.
  • Epithelial tissue forms outer covering or protective lining to internal organs.
  • Connective tissue connects and supports various organs and tissues.
  • Muscular tissue produces voluntary and involuntary movements for locomotion and other movements.
  • Nervous tissue consists of neurons to receive and transmit impulses, regulating activities.
  • Skeletal system protects organs and provides support.
  • Movement occurs by coordination of muscles and bones (musculoskeletal system) under nervous system control.

Important Notes to Remember

  • Roots/shoots grow in length only from tips — cutting tip stops growth.
  • Annual ring count = tree age; width = favourable vs unfavourable year.
  • Xylem parenchyma is the only living part of xylem; phloem is mostly living.
  • Pectin = flexibility (collenchyma); Lignin = hardness (sclerenchyma); Cutin = waterproofing (cuticle).
  • Tendon connects muscle to bone; ligament connects bone to bone — do not interchange.
  • RBCs ~4 months; plasma 55% vs formed 45% — frequent exam numbers.
  • Striated + multinucleate + unbranched = skeletal; spindle + uninucleate + no striations = smooth; branched + faint striations = cardiac.
  • Skull-backbone pivot allows 'no' movement; skull bones fixed allow none; shoulder ball-socket allows all directions; elbow/knee hinge allows one plane.
  • Totipotency terms: dedifferentiation (regain division) → redifferentiation (form roots/shoot).
