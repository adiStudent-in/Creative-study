## 📖 3. Summary (Full — exam version)

### Part I — The dream and the family's opposition

Ever since Sentila was old enough to follow her mother to the fields and forests, she **dreamed of becoming a pot maker** like her mother and grandmother. But her mother **Arenla** wanted her to become a **weaver**. On days when Sentila stayed home while her parents went out, she went to the **expert potters** and watched them at work. They were amused at first, thinking she would "outgrow" her childish passion.

Sentila **did not tell her family** about her love for pot making, because she had **overheard a conversation** between her parents one night. Arenla was complaining about Sentila's indifference to weaving. She said she would not teach her daughter pot making, because it had brought her no joy and only a **pittance**. The riverbank where the grey and red clay is found is **sixteen kilometres** away; she had to climb **down a sheer drop** to the river, and her **back ached** from carrying the heavy load uphill. **Pounding the stubborn clay** inside bamboo cylinders was **tedious**. She often dropped the mould from exhaustion and had to start over. It took **months** to bring out a batch of pots, and the reward was only a few rupees. But weaving paid much better, provided cloth for the family, was **not messy**, and could be done **indoors all seasons** — and one shawl took far less time.

Sentila continued to watch the potters. She saw how the clay was **mixed with water and pounded**, how the potters pushed in their **left hand** into the lump and turned the lump with a **spatula** in the **right hand**. The **tap-tap** of the spatula was like music to her ears. After two–three days the pots were given a final touch, dried in the sun, then placed in a **kiln** on a bed of hay and bamboo and covered. The fire had to be tended carefully, else over/under firing would harm the whole lot.

Arenla **pretended to be unaware** of Sentila's visits; but deep down she noticed the girl. News spread, and the **village council** summoned **Mesoba** to ask why Arenla would not teach her daughter. Mesoba answered humbly: Arenla had never said she would not teach; they wanted Sentila to grow **stronger after her illness**. The elders said pot making **catered to the needs of the people** and **symbolised the tradition and history of the people** — the skill **did not "belong" to any individual**. Experts must pass on their skills both to their own children and to anyone who wished to learn. Mesoba went home and discussed it with Arenla.

### Part II — The real learning

The next year Arenla **took Sentila to the riverbank** and taught her to dig the clay with a **dao**, carry it in a load basket, soak it in the **trough**, and pound it in a **bamboo cylinder**. Sentila was a **quick learner for the dough**, but she could not shape it. When she tried, she could not even hold the lump. Arenla took over and shaped a beautiful pot; these sessions lasted almost a year, but Sentila could not master any of it.

Then, when Sentila was of age, she was sent to sleep in the **girls' dormitory** supervised by a kind **middle-aged widow** whom the girls called **Onula, or Aunty**. Onula had heard of the discord in Sentila's family and decided to help. One evening, Onula watched Sentila trying to make a pot, clumsy and tense; the clay would not obey. When Sentila wearily dropped the misshapen lump, Onula said, "Don't worry, little one, I shall teach you how to make a perfect pot." She demonstrated and gave instructions; Sentila repeated the attempt and **created a beautiful pot**. Onula said only "the mouth is wrong," and told Sentila to watch her mother closely the next time. Sentila did — she watched how the upper hand and spatula moved, how the rhythm ***slackened*** when shaping the mouth, and how a strip of elongated dough made the rim.

One bright day Arenla told Sentila to make as many pots as they could, saying they would not have many sunny days to dry them. As usual Arenla made a batch and asked Sentila to go on. Complaining of a headache and backache, she left — telling Sentila she would try. Sentila, surprised and a little reluctant, began to beat the dough in coordination, and before long she realised the pot was ready. Like a sprinter, she made pot after pot, until she had just **one short of the mother's tally**. She went in to join her mother for lunch — and found her lying on the floor, not breathing. She ran for help. The next morning, when Arenla's body was carried out, Sentila cried, "Mother, I did not wish it to happen this way; it simply came to me. Please forgive me."

On the way, Onula noticed the work-shed door slightly ajar. She stepped in and stopped: **two neat rows of new pots** stood side by side — she could not distinguish one batch from the other. She was sure it was not handiwork of one person. She had witnessed a profound revelation. **A new pot maker was born.**

## 📖 6. Critical Reflection — Extract answers

### Extract 1 (Arenla's speech)
**(i)** The effort is far greater than the returns — **A. The pot making process is quite tiresome and long, and one hardly earns much.**
**(ii)** Arenla wants Sentila to learn weaving because it brings more money and cloth, is not messy, can be done indoors all seasons, and needs less time per piece.
**(iii)** One advantage of weaving over pot making: **it is not messy / can be done indoors in all seasons / return is handsome**.
**(iv)** 'handsome' as in the extract → **B. They will make a handsome profit.**
**(v)** The question mark — **expresses astonishment**: the return·a pittance, compared to all that work, and emphasises the injustice.

### Extract 2 (Onula & Sentila)
**(i)** Sentila's effort is clumsy because **she is too tense** — the clay does not yield the right shape.
**(ii)** Onula is **thoughtful and generous** (C).
**(iii)** The effect (of the clumsy-ness cause) is **A. As a result, the clay seemed unable or unwilling to yield the right shape.**
**(iv)** 'Fashioned' here means **created** (made).
**(v)** Sentila would feel defeated and embarrassed — she let the lump fall and was ready to avoid it, until Onula intercede.

## ✍️ 7. Answer the following — model answers

1. **Describe process of pot making observed by Sentila:** clay mixed with water, pounded; left hand into lump; right hand with spatula rotates & shapes with tap-tap; final touch after 2–3 days ✓ consistency; dried in sun; loaded into kiln on hay/bamboo; covered; fired; care to avoid over/under firing.

2. **Warning given to Mesoba:** the skill must be passed on — the craft is not owned by one person; if only pot makers stop it would be lost; he must remind Arenla to teach both her daughter and others in the community.

3. **Sentila felt** — shame/frustration/humiliation; she hung her head, wanted not to face Arenla, but kept trying because of her passion.

4. **"Onula stood to understand the new phenomenon"** — she realised that the two identical rows of pots meant Sentila could now truly make pots as well as her mother; it was a moment of wonder and of great discovery.

5. **"The tradition and history of the people did not belong to any individual"** — the craft is the identity of the community, handed down the generations; each master is only a caretaker of it. Nobody can claim blocking it; it belongs to all.

6. **Significance of "A new pot maker was born"** — that the craft has a successor; a wholesome new artisan emerges; the tradition will continue, and Sentila has finally achieved her identity. It also implies Arenla's memory lives on in it.

7. **Role of perseverance** — Sentila's dream became real only after repeated failures, patient observation, and her determination. Dreams need hard work and persistence — example of Sentila.

# 🟢 PART B — GIFTS OF GRACE: HONOURING OUR VOCATIONS

## 🏁 At a Glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Poem title** | Gifts of Grace: Honouring Our Vocations |
| **Author** | Anonymous |
| **Type** | Free-verse (no fixed rhyme scheme) patriotic tribute to India's workers |
| **Key idea** | Every worker's craft is a gift; "the voice of their vocation is the voice of their identity" |

## 📜 The Text (as in the book)

> I hear Bharat celebrating, the varied vocations I hear;
> Those of craftsperson, each one celebrating their craft, woven with colours and myriad hues.
> The artisans with lutes, each hailing varied emotions and celebrating dreams, echoing in the streets.
> The carpenters celebrating; they create anything out of wood with mathematical precision,
> The electricians humming; they get ready for work, work with cables and wires to brighten our lives,
> The boatmen gathering their nets from the shore, sailing, and singing while at work, return to tell the tales of life at sea,
> The shoemakers affirming the quality of their work, for the feet that walk, dance, run, jump, return home.
> The delicious singing of the cook, or the rhythm of designer, mason, each celebrating what belongs to them and to none else,
> The voice of their vocation is the voice of their identity. I hear Bharat celebrating, the varied voices I hear!

## 📖 Summary

The poem is a joyful tribute to **every worker of India**. The craftspersons celebrate their colourful work, musicians (artisans with lutes) echo emotions in the streets, carpenters create with mathematical precision, electricians hum while working with cables and wires to brighten our lives, boatmen sing as they sail, shoemakers affirm the quality of their work, and the cook, designer and mason each celebrate what "belongs to them and to none else." The refrain — "I hear Bharat celebrating" — opens and closes the poem, and the key line is: **the voice of their vocation is the voice of their identity.**

## 🎨 Poetic Devices

- **Free verse** — no fixed rhyme scheme; music comes from **repetition and rhythm**.
- **Refrain** — *"I hear Bharat celebrating, the varied vocations I hear"* opens and closes the poem.
- **Repetition** — of "the… voice", "I hear" — creates a chant-like quality.
- **Metaphor** — "the delicious singing of the cook" (the singing is as pleasing as delicious food); "the rhythm of designer, mason" — work as music.
- **Personification** — vocations are given human qualities ("celebrating," "hailing," "humming"); Bharat "celebrating".
- **Sensory imagery (auditory & visual)** — lutes, humming, singing (sound); colours, hues, cables, nets, feet (sight).
- **Alliteration** — e.g., "**v**aried **v**oices", "**m**athematical **m**otion/(precision)".

### Appreciating the poem
1. **Rhyme scheme:** the poem is in **free verse** (no fixed rhyme).
2. **Impact of varying line length:** long lines mirror the **flow of each worker's story**; the varied rhythm makes it song-like and lively.
3. **Pattern in structure:** most lines begin with **"The …"** (the workers) and **end with an action/vocabulary** celebrating them — an **anaphora** (repetition of the opening).
4. **Speaker:** likely a **citizen / poet of India** who listens to and celebrates all workers — a voice of appreciation.
