👤 2. About the Author (general knowledge — not in the textbook)

Sudha Murty is a famous Indian author and social worker, known for her simple, warm stories about everyday people. The story is told in the first person from her own childhood — and at the end we learn the narrator grew up to become a teacher in Computer Science (which Sudha Murty really did).

👥 3. Characters

💡 "Avva" means mother in the Kannada spoken in north Karnataka. The narrator used to call her grandmother Avva.

📖 4. Story Summary — Part I

How the story begins:

  • When the narrator was about twelve, she stayed with her grandparents in a village in north Karnataka.
  • The transport system was not good → the morning paper came only in the afternoon, and the weekly magazine came one day late.
  • Everyone waited eagerly for the bus that brought papers, weekly magazines and the post.

About the writer Triveni:

  • Triveni was a very popular Kannada writer — easy to read, very convincing style.
  • Her stories dealt with complex psychological problems of ordinary people.
  • She died very young, but even after forty years people still appreciate her novels.

The novel Kashi Yatre:

  • One of Triveni's novels, Kashi Yatre, was appearing as a **serial in the Kannada weekly *Karmaveera***.
  • It is the story of an old lady with an ardent (keen) desire to go to Kashi (Varanasi).
  • Most Hindus believe that going to Kashi and worshipping Lord Vishweshwara is the ultimate punya.
  • The old lady struggles to go there. There is also a young orphan girl in the story who falls in love but has no money for her wedding.
  • Ending of the novel: the old lady gives away all her savings without going to Kashi, saying — "The happiness of this orphan girl is more important than worshipping Lord Vishweshwara at Kashi."

The grandmother's habit:

  • Krishtakka never went to school → she could not read.
  • Every Wednesday the magazine came and the narrator read the next episode to her.
  • While listening, she forgot all her work and listened with the greatest concentration; later she could repeat the entire text by heart.
  • The grandmother had also never been to Kashi → she identified herself with the novel's protagonist and was most eager to know what happened next.
  • After hearing the episode, she joined her friends at the temple courtyard and discussed it (while children played hide and seek).

The wedding incident (turning point):

  • The narrator once went to a wedding in a neighbouring village with her cousins. (Those days a wedding was a great event; the elders were busy and children enjoyed their freedom.)
  • She went for a couple of days but stayed a week.
  • When she returned, she saw her grandmother in tears — this surprised her, because she had never seen her cry, even in the most difficult situations.
  • The narrator asked, "Avva, is everything all right?" — Avva nodded but did not reply.
  • That summer night (after dinner), they were sleeping on the open terrace under a full moon. Avva came and sat beside her; her affectionate hands touched the narrator's forehead, and she began to speak.

The grandmother's life story (why she was uneducated):

  • When she was a young girl, she lost her mother and had nobody to look after or guide her.
  • Her father was busy and married again.
  • In those days people never considered education essential for girls, so she never went to school.
  • She married very young, had children, became busy, and later enjoyed cooking and feeding her grandchildren.
  • At times she regretted not going to school — so she made sure her children and grandchildren studied well.

📖 5. Story Summary — Part II

The grandmother's problem:

  • The narrator could not understand why her sixty-two-year-old grandmother was telling her life story in the middle of the night — but she knew she loved her immensely.
  • The grandmother looked unhappy, eyes filled with tears (she was a good-looking lady, usually always smiling).
  • She explained: while the narrator was away, Karmaveera came as usual. She opened the magazine, saw the picture accompanying *Kashi Yatre***, but **could not understand anything written.
  • She rubbed her hands over the pages, wishing they could understand what was written. "If only I was educated enough."
  • She waited eagerly for the narrator to return, and even thought of going to the village to ask her to read — but she was too embarrassed to ask anybody in the village.
  • "I felt very dependent and helpless. We are well-off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?"

The decision:

  • She declared: "I have decided I want to learn the Kannada alphabet from tomorrow onwards."
  • Her deadline: the Saraswati Puja day during Dassara — by that day she should be able to read a novel on her own.
  • "I want to be independent."

The narrator's reaction:

  • The narrator saw her determination but laughed at her childishly: "At this age of sixty-two you want to learn the alphabet? All your hair is grey, your hands are wrinkled, you wear spectacles and you work so much in the kitchen…"
  • The grandmother just smiled: "For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle… For learning there is no age bar."

The learning:

  • From the next day the narrator started her tuition — Avva was a wonderful student; she would read, repeat, write, and recite.
  • The narrator was her only teacher; the grandmother was her first student.
  • (The narrator did not know then that one day she would become a teacher in Computer Science and teach hundreds of students.)

The climax — Dassara:

  • When Dassara came, the narrator secretly bought *Kashi Yatre***, which by then had been **published as a novel.
  • The grandmother called her to the puja place, made her sit on a stool, and gave her a gift of frock material.
  • Then she did something unusual — she bent down and touched the narrator's feet.
  • The narrator was surprised and taken aback: elders never touch the feet of youngsters; we touch the feet of God, elders and teachers as a mark of respect. Today the reverse had happened.
  • The grandmother said: "I am touching the feet of a teacher, not my granddaughter… Now I am independent. It is my duty to respect a teacher. Is it not written in our scriptures that a teacher should be respected, irrespective of the gender and age?"
  • The narrator returned her namaskara (touched her feet) and gave her the gift.
  • The grandmother opened it and read immediately the title "Kashi Yatre" by Triveni and the publisher's name.
  • The narrator knew then that her student had passed with flying colours.

🎯 7. Central Themes & Message

  1. Learning has no age bar — Age, grey hair, or wrinkles are no barrier to learning; one can learn at any age.
  2. Education brings independence — Illiteracy made the grandmother "dependent and helpless"; literacy freed her.
  3. Determination overcomes obstacles — A strong will and hard work can defeat any difficulty.
  4. Respect for teachers and knowledge — A teacher is respected "irrespective of the gender and age"; education deserves the highest honour.
  5. Love and care in a family — The bond between the grandmother and the narrator; the grandmother's sacrifice for her children's education.

💡 Message: No goal is impossible if you are determined — and it is never too late to learn.

📚 10. Grammar — Simple Past vs Past Perfect

The rule (from the story)

  • Past perfect tense (had + V3): the earlier of two completed actions.
  • Simple past tense (V2): the more recent of two completed actions.

The earlier action → past perfect (had + past participle). The more recent action → simple past.

Examples from the story:

  1. "Secretly, I bought *Kashi Yatre which had been published as a novel by that time."*
    • bought = simple past (more recent) · had been published = past perfect (published earlier)
  2. "I knew, then, that my student had passed with flying colours."
    • knew = simple past · had passed = past perfect

More examples:

  • By the time I reached the party, everyone had finished eating.
  • When she returned home, her brother had finished the project.

Model letter (topic: student participation in adult literacy camps)

The Editor The Hindustan Times New Delhi

23rd April, 2026

Subject: Role of Students in Adult Literacy Camps

Sir/Madam,

With reference to the article titled "Illiteracy Still a Hurdle" dated 20th April in your newspaper, I would like to draw your attention towards the alarming number of adults, especially women, who still cannot read or write in our country.

Adult illiteracy affects a large section of society. An illiterate person feels dependent and helpless in everyday life — unable to read a newspaper, sign a form, or understand bank documents. As the story of my grandmother reminds us, "What use is money when I cannot be independent?" Adult literacy camps can end this dependence, and students can play a vital role here. During vacations, students can volunteer to teach basic reading, writing, and numbers to adults in their neighbourhoods.

One effective way to spread awareness is to hold community meetings and street plays that encourage people to join literacy camps. Schools can tie up with organisations and award certificates to student volunteers. Authorities could consider implementing a small service credit for such participation. Such initiatives nurture a sense of responsibility and compassion in students while empowering adults.

I trust this matter will be considered seriously for the benefit of all. I hope this letter gets published in the columns of your esteemed daily.

Yours truly, (Name)

Main Questions — Model Answers

Q1. Why did the grandmother feel embarrassed to ask someone else to read to her while the narrator was away? The grandmother was illiterate and felt dependent and helpless. Asking another villager to read would have openly exposed her inability to read, and she was too embarrassed to admit it. She trusted only her granddaughter, and felt that money could not buy the independence that literacy brings.

Q2. Why does the narrator initially laugh at her grandmother's determination to learn at the age of sixty-two? She laughed childishly, because she thought sixty-two was far too old to begin learning the alphabet. She made fun of the grandmother's grey hair, wrinkled hands and spectacles, forgetting that determination matters more than age. The grandmother's reply — "For learning there is no age bar" — gently corrected her.

Q3. What significance does the story of Kashi Yatre have in both the grandmother's life and the story? Kashi Yatre is the turning point of the story. The grandmother, who had never been to Kashi, identified herself with the old lady protagonist and waited eagerly for each episode. When she could not read the final episodes herself, she felt her illiteracy deeply — which motivated her to learn to read. The narrator also proves her success by gifting her the same novel. Thus the story-within-the-story drives the plot and symbolises the grandmother's struggle for independence.

Q4. What does the grandmother's desire to learn the Kannada alphabet reflect about her? It reflects her strong desire for independence, her determination and will-power, her respect for education, and her belief that learning has no age bar. It also shows her self-respect — she did not want to depend on anyone, even though her family was well-off.

Q5. What lessons can we infer from the grandmother's action of touching the narrator's feet? We learn that a teacher deserves the highest respect, irrespective of the teacher's gender or age. Even though she was the elder, she honoured knowledge above all. It also teaches humility and shows how deeply the grandmother valued the gift of literacy.

Q6. What does the line 'For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle' tell us about the broader theme? It states the central message of the story: determination and a strong will can overcome every obstacle. Age, illiteracy, poverty or difficult circumstances can all be defeated if one is truly determined — here, a sixty-two-year-old woman overcame a lifetime of illiteracy.

Q7. How effectively does the story highlight the value of education in supporting personal independence? Very effectively. The grandmother was "dependent and helpless" without literacy — she could not even read a magazine. Education gave her self-respect, confidence and independence; she could read a whole novel on her own and no longer needed anyone's help. The story proves that education is the key to true independence.

👤 2. About the Poet (general knowledge — not in the textbook)

Subramania Bharati (also known as Mahakavi Bharati) was a great Tamil poet, writer, journalist and freedom fighter of India. He wrote patriotic and devotional poems that inspired people during the freedom struggle.

📜 3. The Poem

Stanza 1 The mighty Himavant is ours— there's no equal anywhere on earth. The generous Ganga is ours— which other river can match her grace? The sacred Upanishads are ours— what scriptures else to name with them? This sunny golden land is ours— she's peerless, let's praise her!

Stanza 2 Gallant warriors have lived here, many a sage has sanctified this land. The divinest music has been heard here, and here all auspicious things are found.

Stanza 3 Here Brahma-knowledge has taken root, and the Buddha preached his dhamma here. Of hoary antiquity is Bharat, she's peerless, let's praise her! …

(Poem text as printed in the textbook; the hyphen/dash at the end of some lines creates a pause.)

📖 4. Summary (Line-by-Line Meaning)

Stanza 1 — India's natural beauty:

  • Himavant (the Himalayas) is ours — no mountain on earth equals it in might. ("Mighty" = its strength and grandeur.)
  • Ganga is ours — no other river can match her grace. (She is called generous because she gives water and life to millions.)
  • The Upanishads (ancient sacred scriptures) are ours — no other scriptures can be named beside them.
  • This sunny golden land is ours — she is peerless (matchless), so let us praise her!

Stanza 2 — India's brave and sacred past:

  • Gallant (brave) warriors have lived here; many sages have sanctified (blessed) this land with their wisdom.
  • The divinest music has been heard here, and all auspicious (fortunate/favourable) things are found here.

Stanza 3 — India's spiritual and philosophical heritage:

  • Brahma-knowledge (spiritual knowledge of the ultimate truth) has taken root here.
  • Buddha preached his dhamma here.
  • Bharat is of hoary antiquity (ancient, time immemorial) — she is peerless; let us praise her!

✅ Summary (Fill-in-the-blanks from the textbook — answers in bold)

The poem is a tribute to India, celebrating its natural beauty, spiritual heritage, and 1. historical greatness. The poet praises the 2. Himavant, describing it as mighty and unparalleled. The 3. Ganga is depicted as generous and graceful, while the 4. Upanishads are honoured as sacred and unmatched. The poem highlights the contributions of 5. sages and 6. warriors who have enriched the land with their bravery and wisdom. It also acknowledges the presence of 7. Brahma-knowledge and the teachings of 8. Buddha, highlighting India's deep-rooted spiritual and philosophical traditions. The poet repeatedly asserts that India is 9. peerless, urging everyone to 10. praise her.

🔎 6. Rhyme Scheme & Structure

  • The poem is written in free verse — it has no fixed rhyme scheme or regular metre.
  • It is a lyrical ode with a refrain that repeats: "she's peerless, let's praise her!"
  • Structure: Stanza 1 (8 lines) presents the "X is ours" pattern (Himavant, Ganga, Upanishads, the land). Stanza 2 (4 lines) celebrates warriors, sages, music and auspicious things. Stanza 3 (4 lines) celebrates Brahma-knowledge, Buddha's dhamma and India's antiquity.

💡 Exam answer: The poem has no fixed rhyme scheme (free verse). The refrain "she's peerless, let's praise her!" gives it a chant-like rhythm.

🛠 7. Literary Devices (with examples) — VERY IMPORTANT

Device Definition Example from the poem
Ode A lyrical poem of deep admiration/praise for a person, place, object or idea; elevated, grand, formal language The whole poem is an ode to India — it praises her "peerless" beauty and greatness
Refrain A line/group of lines repeated regularly "she's peerless, let's praise her!" (repeated at the end of stanza 1 and stanza 3)
Personification Giving human qualities to non-human things Ganga is called "generous"; her movement is "grace" — the river is described like a graceful person. India is addressed as "she"
Rhetorical questions Questions asked for emphasis, not for an answer "Which other river can match her grace?" · "What scriptures else to name with them?" — used to emphasise that India has no equal
Hyperbole Extreme exaggeration (not to be taken literally) "there's no equal anywhere on earth" · "which other river can match her grace?" · "she's peerless"
Allusion An indirect reference to a well-known person/place/book/event Upanishads (ancient scriptures) · Brahma-knowledge (spiritual knowledge/ultimate truth) · Buddha's dhamma (his teachings of compassion and non-violence)
Symbolism Using symbols to suggest deeper ideas Himavant = strength; Ganga = purity/generosity; Upanishads = knowledge; warriors/sages = courage/wisdom; sunny golden land = richness/glory
Imagery Descriptive language that creates vivid mental pictures (appeals to senses) "The mighty Himavant" (visual — towering mountains) · "sunny golden land" (visual — bright, warm) · "divinest music" (auditory — heavenly music)
Hyphens / dashes Create pauses At the end of lines like "The mighty Himavant is ours—" they create deliberate pauses, a chant-like rhythm, and build to the strong assertion after the dash

English — Chapter 1

Class 9 | Two texts: **A)** *How I Taught My Grandmother to Read* (Prose · Sudha Murty) · **B)** *Bharat Our Land* (Poem · Subramania Bharati)

🟣 PART A — HOW I TAUGHT MY GRANDMOTHER TO READ

🎯 THEME

The story is built on three main themes:

  1. Learning has no age bar — a sixty-two-year-old grandmother learns the Kannada alphabet and becomes able to read a whole novel on her own. "For learning there is no age bar."
  2. Education brings independence — illiteracy made the grandmother feel "dependent and helpless"; literacy made her say "Now I am independent." She says, "We are well-off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?"
  3. Determination overcomes every obstacle — "For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle." Strong will defeats age, grey hair, wrinkles and a lifetime of illiteracy.

Underlying values: respect for a teacher, irrespective of the gender and age; the loving bond between grandmother and granddaughter; and the idea that self-respect is worth more than money.

❤️ Message: It is never too late to learn. Education gives confidence, dignity and independence.

📝 LONG ANSWER (Complete summary — exam-ready)

Use this as a complete answer. Every important point of the chapter is here.

"How I Taught My Grandmother to Read" is an autobiographical story by Sudha Murty. When the narrator was a girl of about twelve, she stayed with her grandparents in a village in north Karnataka. In those days the transport system was not good, so the morning paper came only in the afternoon and the weekly magazine came one day late. Everyone waited eagerly for the bus that brought the newspapers, the weekly magazines and the post.

At that time Triveni was a very popular writer in the Kannada language. Her style was easy to read and very convincing. Her stories usually dealt with the complex psychological problems of ordinary people and were always interesting. She died very young, but even after forty years people still appreciate her novels.

One of Triveni's novels, Kashi Yatre, was appearing as a serial in the Kannada weekly Karmaveera. It is the story of an old lady who had an ardent (keen) desire to go to Kashi (Varanasi), because most Hindus believe that going to Kashi and worshipping Lord Vishweshwara is the ultimate punya. There is also a young orphan girl in the story who falls in love but has no money for her wedding. In the end, the old lady gives away all her savings without going to Kashi, saying, "The happiness of this orphan girl is more important than worshipping Lord Vishweshwara at Kashi."

The narrator's grandmother, Krishtakka, never went to school and could not read. Every Wednesday the magazine came and the narrator read the next episode of the story to her. While listening, the grandmother forgot all her work and listened with the greatest concentration, and later she could repeat the entire text by heart. Because she herself had never gone to Kashi, she identified herself with the novel's protagonist and was the most eager of all to know what happened next. After each episode she would discuss it with her friends at the temple courtyard.

Once the narrator went to a wedding in a neighbouring village with her cousins and ended up staying for a week. When she returned, she was surprised to see her grandmother in tears, because she had never seen her cry even in the most difficult situations. That summer night, while the family slept on the open terrace under a full moon, the grandmother, whom the narrator called Avva (meaning "mother" in the Kannada of north Karnataka), told her the story of her life: she lost her mother young, her busy father married again, and in those days people never considered education essential for girls — so she never went to school. She regretted it and made sure her own children grew up well.

Then Avva explained why she was crying. When the narrator was away, Karmaveera had come. She opened the magazine and saw the picture that accompanies the story, but she could not understand anything written. She became "dependent and helpless." She said, "We are well off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?" So she decided to learn the Kannada alphabet from the very next day, keeping the Saraswati Puja day during Dassara as the deadline — by that day she must be able to read a novel on her own.

The narrator laughed childishly at her — at sixty-two, grey hair, wrinkled hands, spectacles, so much kitchen work, learning the alphabet? But the grandmother just smiled and said, "For a good cause if you are determined you can overcome any obstacle. I will work harder than anybody. For learning there is no age bar." From the next day the narrator started her tuition. The grandmother proved to be a wonderful student — she read, repeated, wrote and recited. The narrator was her only teacher, and the grandmother was her first student. (The narrator did not suspect then that one day she would become a teacher of Computer Science.)

On Dassara, the narrator bought Kashi Yatre secretly, as the novel had by then been published as a book. The grandmother called her to the puja place, made her sit on a stool, gave her a gift of frock material, and then bent down and touched her feet. The narrator was taken aback — elders never touch the feet of youngsters; we touch the feet of God, elders and teachers as a mark of respect. The grandmother said, "I am touching the feet of a teacher, not my granddaughter … Now I am independent. It is my duty to respect a teacher. Is it not written in our scriptures that a teacher should be respected, irrespective of the gender and age?" The narrator returned her namaskara and gave her the gift. The grandmother opened it and read the title Kashi Yatre by Triveni and the publisher's name, and the narrator knew that her student had passed with flying colours.

❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (with answers)

1. The phrase "never seen her cry in the most difficult situations" tells us the grandmother was — → A. strong-willed. It shows her emotional strength, not kindness, thoughtfulness or daring.

2. When the grandmother did not answer, it was because she was too ______ (emotional / tired) to speak. → emotional. She was about to reveal the painful secret of her illiteracy.

3. "I want to be independent" reveals about her character — → B. She desires self-sufficiency. She does not want to prove her intelligence, nor is she pressured by society — she simply wants freedom.

4. Her determination shows that learning has no — → age limit. She is sixty-two, yet she learns the alphabet from zero.

5. Why did the narrator laugh when the grandmother decided to learn? Because she judged her grandmother by age and appearance — grey hair, wrinkled hands, spectacles, and so much work in the kitchen — and she thought a person of sixty-two could never begin the alphabet. It is a childish reaction; her grandmother's gentle answer — "For learning there is no age bar" — corrects her.

6. Why was the grandmother embarrassed to ask someone else to read? She felt ashamed of her illiteracy. She would rather wait for her granddaughter than let the people know she could not read. She said she felt "dependent and helpless" and waiting for the narrator was her own chosen dependence.

7. Why did she set the deadline of Saraswati Puja during Dassara? Saraswati is the goddess of learning; Dassara is a festival of knowledge and victory. Choosing that day to read a novel symbolises her learning as a sacred achievement and gives her a firm meaningful goal to work towards.

8. Why did the grandmother touch the narrator's feet? By tradition, only the feet of God, elders and teachers are touched — never a younger person's. She touched her granddaughter's feet because she honoured the teacher, not the grandchild. She says the scriptures say a teacher is to be respected "irrespective of gender and age." Her act shows knowledge honours above everything.

9. The main lesson of the story is — no age is too old to learn, education is freedom, determination wins, and a teacher deserves respect at any age.

10. Why did the picture in the magazine shock the grandmother? She opened the magazine, saw the picture that goes with the story, and could not read a single word beside it. That moment of helplessness, when she rubbed her hands over the pages, prompted her to decide to learn.

🟢 PART B — BHARAT OUR LAND (POEM)

🎭 THEME

Bharat Our Land by Subramania Bharati is a patriotic ode to India's natural beauty, spiritual heritage, valour and ancient glory. The poet celebrates the Himalayas (Himavant), the Ganga, the Upanishads, warriors, sages, divine music, Brahma-Knowledge and Buddha's Dhamma, and repeats like a refrain: India is peerless — "she's peerless, let's praise her!" — to awaken love, pride and devotion for the homeland.

💡 CENTRAL IDEA

India is unmatched among all the lands of the earth. It is a force of nature — the mighty Himalayas and the graceful, generous Ganga — as well as the treasure of the Upanishads, the valour of warriors, the purity of sages, divine music, Brahma-knowledge and the Dhamma of the Buddha. Since it is ancient (of hoary antiquity) and peerless, the poet urges all of us to praise it with love and pride.

📜 STANZA-WISE EXPLANATION (correct & complete)

Stanza 1 — What belongs to us

The mighty Himavant is ours — there's no equal anywhere on earth. The generous Ganga is ours — which other river can match her grace? The sacred Upanishads are ours — what scriptures else to name with them? This sunny golden land is ours — she's peerless, let's praise her!

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The poet proudly lists India's natural treasures and claims them as "ours".
  • The word mighty shows the Himavant (the Himalayas) is great in strength and grandeur. "No equal anywhere on earth" is an exaggeration / hyperbole.
  • The Ganga, like a generous mother, gives water and life to the land — she is personified. Her grace speaks of her gentle, beautiful form. The rhetorical question ("which other river can match her grace?") does not expect an answer — it simply emphasises that none can.
  • The Upanishads (our oldest sacred texts) have unmatched wisdom — what other scriptures equal them?
  • "This sunny golden land" is India — metaphor: our country is bright, prosperous and holy.
  • The refrain "she's peerless, let's praise her!" ends the stanza and lifts the note of praise and pride.

Stanza 2 — Valour and sanctity

Gallant warriors have lived here, many a sage has sanctified this land. The divinest music has been heard here, and here all auspicious things are found.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • Gallant warriors — the brave heroes of our land — have lived here. By their presence and their deeds the land becomes historic.
  • Sages have made the land sacred / holy by their prayers and their penance — they have "sanctified this land".
  • Divine music — the greatest music has been heard in this land (the poet calls it "the divinest music").
  • All the auspicious things — everything good, holy and favourable — are at home in India.
  • Tone: proud, reverent, reverential.

Stanza 3 — The oldest wisdom and the eternal Bharat

Here Brahma-knowledge has taken root, and the Buddha preached his dhamma here. Of hoary antiquity is Bharat, she's peerless, let's praise her! …

Meaning/Explanation:

  • Brahma-knowledge (the supreme spiritual knowledge connected to self-realisation and the ultimate truth, as the textbook explains) has "taken root" in this land — India is the home of deep philosophy and enlightenment.
  • The Buddha preached his Dhamma here — Gautam Buddha taught his path of compassion, non-violence and enlightenment in this very land.
  • "Of hoary antiquity is Bharat" — Bharat (India) is very ancient. Hoary means white or grey with age; antiquity means ancient times. India has a long, unbroken and honourable history.
  • The refrain "she's peerless, let's praise her" returns and ends the poem with the same ring of praise.

Note on the poem: The refrain repeats, giving the poem a musical, chant-like shape. India is called "she", i.e. personified as a mother and a goddess, so the poem speaks to the land with love.
