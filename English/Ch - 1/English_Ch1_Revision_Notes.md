## 📖 4. Story Summary — Part I

**How the story begins:**
- When the narrator was about **twelve**, she stayed with her **grandparents in a village in north Karnataka**.
- The **transport system was not good** → the morning paper came only in the **afternoon**, and the weekly magazine came **one day late**.
- Everyone waited eagerly for the **bus** that brought papers, weekly magazines and the **post**.

**About the writer Triveni:**
- Triveni was a **very popular Kannada writer** — easy to read, very **convincing** style.
- Her stories dealt with **complex psychological problems of ordinary people**.
- She **died very young**, but even **after forty years** people still appreciate her novels.

**The novel *Kashi Yatre*:**
- One of Triveni's novels, *Kashi Yatre*, was appearing as a **serial in the Kannada weekly *Karmaveera***.
- It is the story of an **old lady** with an **ardent (keen) desire to go to Kashi (Varanasi)**.
- Most Hindus believe that going to Kashi and worshipping **Lord Vishweshwara** is the **ultimate punya**.
- The old lady struggles to go there. There is also a **young orphan girl** in the story who falls in love but has **no money for her wedding**.
- **Ending of the novel:** the old lady **gives away all her savings without going to Kashi**, saying — *"The happiness of this orphan girl is more important than worshipping Lord Vishweshwara at Kashi."*

**The grandmother's habit:**
- Krishtakka **never went to school** → she **could not read**.
- **Every Wednesday** the magazine came and the narrator read the **next episode** to her.
- While listening, she forgot all her work and listened with the **greatest concentration**; later she could **repeat the entire text by heart**.
- The grandmother had also **never been to Kashi** → she **identified herself with the novel's protagonist** and was most eager to know what happened next.
- After hearing the episode, she joined her **friends at the temple courtyard** and discussed it (while children played **hide and seek**).

**The wedding incident (turning point):**
- The narrator once went to a **wedding in a neighbouring village** with her cousins. (Those days a wedding was a great event; the elders were busy and children enjoyed their freedom.)
- She went for **a couple of days but stayed a week**.
- When she returned, she saw her grandmother **in tears** — this surprised her, because she had **never seen her cry, even in the most difficult situations**.
- The narrator asked, *"Avva, is everything all right?"* — Avva **nodded but did not reply**.
- That **summer night** (after dinner), they were sleeping on the **open terrace** under a **full moon**. Avva came and sat beside her; her **affectionate hands touched the narrator's forehead**, and she began to speak.

**The grandmother's life story (why she was uneducated):**
- When she was a young girl, she **lost her mother** and had nobody to look after or guide her.
- Her father was busy and **married again**.
- **In those days people never considered education essential for girls**, so she never went to school.
- She married **very young**, had children, became busy, and later enjoyed **cooking and feeding her grandchildren**.
- At times she **regretted not going to school** — so she **made sure her children and grandchildren studied well**.

---

## 📖 5. Story Summary — Part II

**The grandmother's problem:**
- The narrator could not understand why her **sixty-two-year-old** grandmother was telling her life story in the middle of the night — but she knew she loved her immensely.
- The grandmother looked **unhappy, eyes filled with tears** (she was a good-looking lady, **usually always smiling**).
- She explained: while the narrator was away, *Karmaveera* came as usual. She opened the magazine, saw the **picture accompanying *Kashi Yatre***, but **could not understand anything written**.
- She **rubbed her hands over the pages**, wishing they could understand what was written. *"If only I was educated enough."*
- She waited eagerly for the narrator to return, and even thought of **going to the village** to ask her to read — but she was **too embarrassed** to ask anybody in the village.
- *"I felt very dependent and helpless. We are well-off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?"*

**The decision:**
- She declared: **"I have decided I want to learn the Kannada alphabet from tomorrow onwards."**
- Her deadline: the **Saraswati Puja day during Dassara** — by that day she should be able to **read a novel on her own**.
- **"I want to be independent."**

**The narrator's reaction:**
- The narrator saw her determination but **laughed at her** childishly: *"At this age of sixty-two you want to learn the alphabet? All your hair is grey, your hands are wrinkled, you wear spectacles and you work so much in the kitchen…"*
- The grandmother just smiled: **"For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle… For learning there is no age bar."**

**The learning:**
- From the next day the narrator **started her tuition** — Avva was a **wonderful student**; she would **read, repeat, write, and recite**.
- The narrator was her **only teacher**; the grandmother was her **first student**.
- *(The narrator did not know then that one day she would become a **teacher in Computer Science** and teach hundreds of students.)*

**The climax — Dassara:**
- When Dassara came, the narrator **secretly bought *Kashi Yatre***, which by then had been **published as a novel**.
- The grandmother called her to the **puja place**, made her sit on a stool, and gave her a **gift of frock material**.
- Then she did something unusual — she **bent down and touched the narrator's feet**.
- The narrator was **surprised and taken aback**: elders never touch the feet of youngsters; we touch the feet of **God, elders and teachers** as a mark of respect. Today the reverse had happened.
- The grandmother said: *"I am touching the feet of a teacher, not my granddaughter… Now I am independent. It is my duty to respect a teacher. Is it not written in our scriptures that a teacher should be respected, irrespective of the gender and age?"*
- The narrator **returned her namaskara** (touched her feet) and gave her the gift.
- The grandmother **opened it and read immediately the title "Kashi Yatre" by Triveni and the publisher's name**.
- The narrator knew then that **her student had passed with flying colours**.

### A. Binomials (two words joined by 'and'/'or', used in fixed order)

> **Definition:** Binomials are expressions of **two words usually linked by 'and' or 'or'**, used together in a fixed order, e.g., *odds and ends*, *right or wrong*, *hide and seek*.

|---|---|
| **sink or swim** | succeed or fail without help |
| **on and off** | sometimes, occasionally |
| **mix and match** | put different things together to get a range of possibilities |
| **all or nothing** | something to be done completely or not at all |
| **part and parcel** | complete part of / belonging to |
| **pick and choose** | choose only the best |
| **sooner or later** | at some time in the future |
| **leaps and bounds** | increase or develop very quickly |

**Example sentences:**

## 📚 10. Grammar — Simple Past vs Past Perfect

### The rule (from the story)
- **Past perfect tense (had + V3):** the **earlier** of two completed actions.
- **Simple past tense (V2):** the **more recent** of two completed actions.

> The **earlier action** → past perfect (had + past participle).
> The **more recent action** → simple past.

**Examples from the story:**
1. *"Secretly, I **bought** *Kashi Yatre* which **had been published** as a novel by that time."*
   - `bought` = **simple past** (more recent) · `had been published` = **past perfect** (published earlier)
2. *"I **knew**, then, that my student **had passed** with flying colours."*
   - `knew` = **simple past** · `had passed` = **past perfect**

**More examples:**
- By the time I reached the party, everyone **had finished** eating.
- When she returned home, her brother **had finished** the project.

### Critical Reflection — Extract 1

**Extract:** *"When I came back to my village, I saw my grandmother in tears. I was surprised, for I had never seen her cry even in the most difficult situations…"*

**(i)** The phrase "never seen her cry in the most difficult situations" tells us that the grandmother was **→ A. strong-willed**.
**(ii)** Grandmother did not reply because she might have been too **emotional** to respond.
**(iii)** Clue of a **rural setting with traditional customs**: they slept on the **open terrace** of the house on a summer night; the affectionate gesture of touching the forehead; the traditional Kannada address **"Avva"**.
**(iv)** Lines that establish a **tender atmosphere**: *"Avva came and sat next to me. Her affectionate hands touched my forehead."* (the warm summer night with the full moon adds to this).
**(v)** The aspect **NOT emphasised** in this extract: **→ C. the grandmother's regret over her lack of education.** (The extract shows the grandmother's emotional turmoil, the affectionate bond, and the narrator's concern — not her regret over education.)

### Critical Reflection — Extract 2

**Extract:** *"I have decided I want to learn the Kannada alphabet from tomorrow onwards… I want to be independent."*

**(i)** "I want to be independent" reveals that she **→ B. desires self-sufficiency**.
**(ii)** The grandmother's determination shows that learning has no **age limit**.
**(iii)** The narrator laughs because **she thought sixty-two was too old to learn the alphabet** — she childishly judged her grandmother by her grey hair, wrinkled hands, spectacles, and kitchen work, instead of her determination.
**(iv)** Any **two qualities**: **determination / will-power** and **humility / respect for teachers** (also: hard work, self-respect).
**(v)** The narrator is making assumptions because **she judged her grandmother by her age and appearance** and assumed an old lady could not learn — but the grandmother proved her wrong with her hard work.

### Main Questions — Model Answers

**Q1. Why did the grandmother feel embarrassed to ask someone else to read to her while the narrator was away?**
The grandmother was illiterate and felt **dependent and helpless**. Asking another villager to read would have openly exposed her inability to read, and she was **too embarrassed to admit it**. She trusted only her granddaughter, and felt that money could not buy the independence that literacy brings.

**Q2. Why does the narrator initially laugh at her grandmother's determination to learn at the age of sixty-two?**
She laughed **childishly**, because she thought sixty-two was far too old to begin learning the alphabet. She made fun of the grandmother's grey hair, wrinkled hands and spectacles, forgetting that determination matters more than age. The grandmother's reply — "For learning there is no age bar" — gently corrected her.

**Q3. What significance does the story of *Kashi Yatre* have in both the grandmother's life and the story?**
*Kashi Yatre* is the **turning point** of the story. The grandmother, who had never been to Kashi, **identified herself with the old lady protagonist** and waited eagerly for each episode. When she could not read the final episodes herself, she felt her illiteracy deeply — which **motivated her to learn to read**. The narrator also proves her success by gifting her the same novel. Thus the story-within-the-story drives the plot and symbolises the grandmother's struggle for independence.

**Q4. What does the grandmother's desire to learn the Kannada alphabet reflect about her?**
It reflects her **strong desire for independence**, her **determination and will-power**, her **respect for education**, and her belief that **learning has no age bar**. It also shows her self-respect — she did not want to depend on anyone, even though her family was well-off.

**Q5. What lessons can we infer from the grandmother's action of touching the narrator's feet?**
We learn that **a teacher deserves the highest respect, irrespective of the teacher's gender or age**. Even though she was the elder, she honoured knowledge above all. It also teaches **humility** and shows how deeply the grandmother valued the gift of literacy.

**Q6. What does the line 'For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle' tell us about the broader theme?**
It states the **central message** of the story: **determination and a strong will can overcome every obstacle**. Age, illiteracy, poverty or difficult circumstances can all be defeated if one is truly determined — here, a sixty-two-year-old woman overcame a lifetime of illiteracy.

**Q7. How effectively does the story highlight the value of education in supporting personal independence?**
Very effectively. The grandmother was "dependent and helpless" without literacy — she could not even read a magazine. Education gave her **self-respect, confidence and independence**; she could read a whole novel on her own and no longer needed anyone's help. The story proves that education is the key to true independence.

## 📖 4. Summary (Line-by-Line Meaning)

**Stanza 1 — India's natural beauty:**
- **Himavant (the Himalayas)** is ours — no mountain on earth equals it in might. ("Mighty" = its strength and grandeur.)
- **Ganga** is ours — no other river can match her **grace**. (She is called **generous** because she gives water and life to millions.)
- The **Upanishads** (ancient sacred scriptures) are ours — no other scriptures can be named beside them.
- This **sunny golden land** is ours — she is **peerless (matchless)**, so let us praise her!

**Stanza 2 — India's brave and sacred past:**
- **Gallant (brave) warriors** have lived here; **many sages** have **sanctified (blessed)** this land with their wisdom.
- The **divinest music** has been heard here, and all **auspicious (fortunate/favourable) things** are found here.

**Stanza 3 — India's spiritual and philosophical heritage:**
- **Brahma-knowledge** (spiritual knowledge of the ultimate truth) has taken root here.
- **Buddha preached his dhamma** here.
- Bharat is of **hoary antiquity** (ancient, time immemorial) — she is peerless; let us praise her!

## 🛠 7. Literary Devices (with examples) — VERY IMPORTANT

| Device | Definition | Example from the poem |
|---|---|---|
| **Ode** | A lyrical poem of deep admiration/praise for a person, place, object or idea; elevated, grand, formal language | The whole poem is an ode to **India** — it praises her "peerless" beauty and greatness |
| **Refrain** | A line/group of lines repeated regularly | **"she's peerless, let's praise her!"** (repeated at the end of stanza 1 and stanza 3) |
| **Personification** | Giving human qualities to non-human things | Ganga is called **"generous"**; her movement is **"grace"** — the river is described like a graceful person. India is addressed as **"she"** |
| **Rhetorical questions** | Questions asked for emphasis, not for an answer | *"Which other river can match her grace?"* · *"What scriptures else to name with them?"* — used to **emphasise** that India has no equal |
| **Hyperbole** | Extreme exaggeration (not to be taken literally) | *"there's no equal anywhere on earth"* · *"which other river can match her grace?"* · *"she's peerless"* |
| **Allusion** | An indirect reference to a well-known person/place/book/event | **Upanishads** (ancient scriptures) · **Brahma-knowledge** (spiritual knowledge/ultimate truth) · **Buddha's dhamma** (his teachings of compassion and non-violence) |
| **Symbolism** | Using symbols to suggest deeper ideas | Himavant = strength; Ganga = purity/generosity; Upanishads = knowledge; warriors/sages = courage/wisdom; sunny golden land = richness/glory |
| **Imagery** | Descriptive language that creates vivid mental pictures (appeals to senses) | *"The mighty Himavant"* (visual — towering mountains) · *"sunny golden land"* (visual — bright, warm) · *"divinest music"* (auditory — heavenly music) |
| **Hyphens / dashes** | Create pauses | At the end of lines like *"The mighty Himavant is ours—"* they create deliberate pauses, a chant-like rhythm, and build to the strong assertion after the dash |

### Critical Reflection (Extract) — Answers

**Extract:** *The mighty Himavant is ours— / there's no equal anywhere on earth. / The generous Ganga is ours— / which other river can match her grace? / The sacred Upanishads are ours— / what scriptures else to name with them?*

1. The word "mighty" refers to the **strength / grandeur** of the Himavant.
2. The question mark at the end of the fourth and sixth lines is used to **(ii) emphasise a point** (rhetorical questions).
3. The Ganga is called **"generous"** because she **gives life, water and nourishment to millions** — like a kind giver who shares freely with all.
4. The river's movement is described as **graceful** because its gentle, flowing, winding motion is beautiful and elegant — like a graceful person (personification).
5. The poet implies that the Upanishads are **unmatched** in their wisdom and spiritual depth.

### Main Questions — Model Answers

**Q1. How does the poem reflect a strong connection to cultural identity and heritage?**
The poem celebrates India's **mountains (Himavant), rivers (Ganga), sacred scriptures (Upanishads), brave warriors, sages, divine music, Brahma-knowledge and Buddha's dhamma**. All these are elements of our **cultural and spiritual heritage**. By calling the land "ours" and "peerless," the poet makes every Indian feel proud of this shared identity and heritage.

**Q2. What can you infer about the poet's attitude towards India from the repeated phrase 'she's peerless, let's praise her!'?**
The repetition shows the poet's **deep love, pride and reverence** for India. He believes India is **matchless** — in beauty, wisdom and glory — and he wants every reader to feel the same pride and join in praising her.

**Q3. What does the line 'many a sage has sanctified this land' suggest about India's spiritual heritage?**
It suggests that India's spiritual heritage is **ancient, sacred and rich**. Many sages have lived here, and their wisdom, penance and teachings have **blessed and purified** the land, making it a holy place of knowledge and enlightenment.

**Q4. How does the poet connect warriors and music to India's greatness?**
**Warriors** gave the land **valour and protection** — they defended it with courage. **Music** (the "divinest music") gave it **art, culture and beauty**. Together they show that India is great in both **strength (heroism)** and **culture (art and wisdom)** — every noble quality is found here.

**Q5. How does this poem foster a sense of national pride?**
The poem lists India's matchless treasures — mighty mountains, generous rivers, sacred scriptures, brave heroes, wise sages and divine knowledge — and repeats that she is **peerless**. This **uplifts the reader**, creates pride in our heritage, and inspires love and devotion for the motherland.
