# 🟣 PART A — HOW I TAUGHT MY GRANDMOTHER TO READ

## 🎯 THEME

The story is built on **three main themes**:

1. **Learning has no age bar** — a sixty-two-year-old grandmother learns the Kannada alphabet and becomes able to read a whole novel on her own. *"For learning there is no age bar."*
2. **Education brings independence** — illiteracy made the grandmother feel "dependent and helpless"; literacy made her say *"Now I am independent."* She says, *"We are well-off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?"*
3. **Determination overcomes every obstacle** — *"For a good cause if you are determined, you can overcome any obstacle."* Strong will defeats age, grey hair, wrinkles and a lifetime of illiteracy.

**Underlying values:** respect for a teacher, *irrespective of the gender and age*; the loving bond between grandmother and granddaughter; and the idea that self-respect is worth more than money.

> ❤️ **Message:** *It is never too late to learn. Education gives confidence, dignity and independence.*

---

## 📝 LONG ANSWER (Complete summary — exam-ready)

> *Use this as a complete answer. Every important point of the chapter is here.*

**"How I Taught My Grandmother to Read" is an autobiographical story by Sudha Murty.** When the narrator was a girl of about twelve, she stayed with her grandparents in a village in **north Karnataka**. In those days the transport system was not good, so the morning paper came only in the afternoon and the weekly magazine came one day late. Everyone waited eagerly for the bus that brought the newspapers, the weekly magazines and the post.

At that time **Triveni** was a very popular writer in the **Kannada** language. Her style was easy to read and very **convincing**. Her stories usually dealt with the complex psychological problems of ordinary people and were always interesting. She died very young, but even after forty years people still appreciate her novels.

One of Triveni's novels, **Kashi Yatre**, was appearing as a serial in the Kannada weekly **Karmaveera**. It is the story of an old lady who had an **ardent** (keen) desire to go to **Kashi** (Varanasi), because most Hindus believe that going to Kashi and worshipping **Lord Vishweshwara** is the ultimate punya. There is also a young orphan girl in the story who falls in love but has no money for her wedding. In the end, the old lady gives away all her savings without going to Kashi, saying, *"The happiness of this orphan girl is more important than worshipping Lord Vishweshwara at Kashi."*

The narrator's grandmother, **Krishtakka**, never went to school and could not read. Every **Wednesday** the magazine came and the narrator read the next episode of the story to her. While listening, the grandmother forgot all her work and listened with the greatest **concentration**, and later she could repeat the entire text **by heart**. Because she herself had never gone to Kashi, she identified herself with the novel's protagonist and was the most eager of all to know what happened next. After each episode she would discuss it with her friends at the temple courtyard.

Once the narrator went to a wedding in a neighbouring village with her cousins and ended up staying for a week. When she returned, she was surprised to see her grandmother in tears, because she had never seen her cry even in the most difficult situations. That summer night, while the family slept on the open terrace under a full moon, the grandmother, whom the narrator called **Avva** (meaning "mother" in the Kannada of north Karnataka), told her the story of her life: she lost her mother young, her busy father married again, and in those days people never considered education essential for girls — so she never went to school. She regretted it and made sure her own children grew up well.

Then Avva explained why she was crying. When the narrator was away, *Karmaveera* had come. She opened the magazine and saw the picture that accompanies the story, but she could not understand anything written. She became "dependent and helpless." She said, *"We are well off, but what use is money when I cannot be independent?"* So she decided to **learn the Kannada alphabet from the very next day**, keeping the **Saraswati Puja day during Dassara** as the deadline — by that day she must be able to read a novel on her own.

The narrator laughed childishly at her — at sixty-two, grey hair, wrinkled hands, spectacles, so much kitchen work, learning the alphabet? But the grandmother just smiled and said, *"For a good cause if you are determined you can overcome any obstacle. I will work harder than anybody. For learning there is no age bar."* From the next day the narrator started her tuition. The grandmother proved to be a wonderful student — she read, repeated, wrote and recited. The narrator was her only teacher, and the grandmother was her first student. (The narrator did not suspect then that one day she would become a teacher of Computer Science.)

On Dassara, the narrator bought *Kashi Yatre* secretly, as the novel had by then been published as a book. The grandmother called her to the puja place, made her sit on a stool, gave her a gift of frock material, and then bent down and touched her feet. The narrator was taken aback — elders never touch the feet of youngsters; we touch the feet of God, elders and teachers as a mark of respect. The grandmother said, *"I am touching the feet of a teacher, not my granddaughter … Now I am independent. It is my duty to respect a teacher. Is it not written in our scriptures that a teacher should be respected, irrespective of the gender and age?"* The narrator returned her namaskara and gave her the gift. The grandmother opened it and read the title *Kashi Yatre* by Triveni and the publisher's name, and the narrator knew that her student had **passed with flying colours**.

## 👥 CHARACTER SKETCHES

### The Grandmother — Krishtakka (Avva)
She is a **strong-willed, determined** lady of **sixty-two**. She never went to school, so she cannot read, a helplessness she deeply felt. Her inner strength is remarkable — she sets her own deadline (Saraswati Puja during Dassara) and works harder than anyone, reading, repeating, writing and reciting. She has **great self-respect**: money could not replace her independence. She is also **humble and respectful** — she touches her granddaughter's feet to honour her teacher. She is **loving** — she fed her grandchildren with happiness and made sure they studied well. Her words *"For learning there is no age bar"* are the soul of the story.

### The narrator (young Sudha Murty)
A twelve-year-old girl living with her grandparents in north Karnataka. She is **loving and patient** — she reads the magazine to her grandmother every Wednesday and later teaches her the entire alphabet with care. She is kind and caring (she is worried and upset when she sees her grandmother in tears), though at one point she is childishly thoughtless (she laughs at her grandmother's age). She grows into a good teacher, proud that her first student passed "with flying colours." She later becomes a teacher in Computer Science.

### Triveni
The **popular Kannada novelist** whose book is the heart of the story. She wrote easy, convincing stories about the psychological problems of ordinary people. She **died young**, yet even after forty years her novels are still appreciated. Her novel *Kashi Yatre* brings the grandmother and the narrator together and sets the story in motion.

## ❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (with answers)

**1. The phrase "never seen her cry in the most difficult situations" tells us the grandmother was —**
**→ A. strong-willed.** It shows her emotional strength, not kindness, thoughtfulness or daring.

**2. When the grandmother did not answer, it was because she was too ______ (emotional / tired) to speak.**
**→ emotional.** She was about to reveal the painful secret of her illiteracy.

**3. "I want to be independent" reveals about her character —**
**→ B. She desires self-sufficiency.** She does not want to prove her intelligence, nor is she pressured by society — she simply wants freedom.

**4. Her determination shows that learning has no —**
**→ age limit.** She is sixty-two, yet she learns the alphabet from zero.

**5. Why did the narrator laugh when the grandmother decided to learn?**
Because she judged her grandmother by **age and appearance** — grey hair, wrinkled hands, spectacles, and so much work in the kitchen — and she thought a person of sixty-two could never begin the alphabet. It is a childish reaction; her grandmother's gentle answer — *"For learning there is no age bar"* — corrects her.

**6. Why was the grandmother embarrassed to ask someone else to read?**
She felt ashamed of her illiteracy. She would rather wait for her granddaughter than let the people know she could not read. She said she felt "dependent and helpless" and waiting for the narrator was her own chosen dependence.

**7. Why did she set the deadline of Saraswati Puja during Dassara?**
Saraswati is the goddess of learning; Dassara is a festival of knowledge and victory. Choosing that day to read a novel **symbolises** her learning as a **sacred achievement** and gives her a firm meaningful goal to work towards.

**8. Why did the grandmother touch the narrator's feet?**
By tradition, only the feet of **God, elders and teachers** are touched — never a younger person's. She touched her granddaughter's feet because she honoured the **teacher**, not the grandchild. She says the scriptures say a teacher is to be respected *"irrespective of gender and age."* Her act shows **knowledge honours above everything**.

**9. The main lesson of the story is —** no age is too old to learn, education is freedom, determination wins, and a teacher deserves respect at any age.

**10. Why did the picture in the magazine shock the grandmother?**
She opened the magazine, saw the picture that goes with the story, and could not read a single word beside it. That moment of helplessness, when she rubbed her hands over the pages, prompted her to decide to learn.

# 🟢 PART B — BHARAT OUR LAND (POEM)

## 🎭 THEME

**Bharat Our Land by Subramania Bharati** is a patriotic **ode** to India's **natural beauty, spiritual heritage, valour and ancient glory**. The poet celebrates the Himalayas (Himavant), the Ganga, the Upanishads, warriors, sages, divine music, Brahma-Knowledge and Buddha's Dhamma, and repeats like a refrain: **India is peerless — "she's peerless, let's praise her!"** — to awaken love, pride and devotion for the homeland.

---

## 💡 CENTRAL IDEA

**India is unmatched among all the lands of the earth.** It is a force of nature — the mighty Himalayas and the graceful, generous Ganga — as well as the treasure of the Upanishads, the valour of warriors, the purity of sages, divine music, Brahma-knowledge and the Dhamma of the Buddha. Since it is ancient (of hoary antiquity) and peerless, the poet urges all of us to praise it with love and pride.

---

## 📜 STANZA-WISE EXPLANATION (correct & complete)

### Stanza 1 — What belongs to us

> The mighty Himavant is ours —
> there's no equal anywhere on earth.
> The generous Ganga is ours —
> which other river can match her grace?
> The sacred Upanishads are ours —
> what scriptures else to name with them?
> This sunny golden land is ours —
> she's peerless, let's praise her!

**Meaning/Explanation:**
- The poet proudly lists **India's natural treasures** and claims them as "ours".
- The word **mighty** shows the Himavant (the Himalayas) is great in **strength and grandeur**. "No equal anywhere on earth" is an **exaggeration / hyperbole**.
- The **Ganga**, like a **generous mother**, gives water and life to the land — she is **personified**. Her **grace** speaks of her gentle, beautiful form. The **rhetorical question** ("which other river can match her grace?") does not expect an answer — it simply emphasises that none can.
- **The Upanishads** (our oldest sacred texts) have unmatched wisdom — *what other scriptures equal them?*
- **"This sunny golden land"** is India — **metaphor**: our country is bright, prosperous and holy.
- The refrain **"she's peerless, let's praise her!"** ends the stanza and lifts the note of praise and pride.

### Stanza 2 — Valour and sanctity

> Gallant warriors have lived here,
> many a sage has sanctified this land.
> The divinest music has been heard here,
> and here all auspicious things are found.

**Meaning/Explanation:**
- **Gallant warriors** — the brave heroes of our land — have lived here. By their presence and their deeds the land becomes historic.
- **Sages** have made the land **sacred / holy** by their prayers and their penance — they have **"sanctified this land"**.
- **Divine music** — the greatest music has been heard in this land (the poet calls it "the divinest music").
- **All the auspicious things** — everything good, holy and favourable — are at home in India.
- Tone: **proud, reverent, reverential**.

### Stanza 3 — The oldest wisdom and the eternal Bharat

> Here Brahma-knowledge has taken root,
> and the Buddha preached his dhamma here.
> Of hoary antiquity is Bharat,
> she's peerless, let's praise her! …

**Meaning/Explanation:**
- **Brahma-knowledge** (the supreme spiritual knowledge connected to self-realisation and the ultimate truth, as the textbook explains) has "taken root" in this land — India is the home of deep philosophy and enlightenment.
- **The Buddha preached his Dhamma here** — Gautam Buddha taught his path of **compassion, non-violence and enlightenment** in this very land.
- **"Of hoary antiquity is Bharat"** — Bharat (India) is **very ancient**. *Hoary* means white or grey with age; *antiquity* means ancient times. India has a long, unbroken and honourable history.
- The refrain *"she's peerless, let's praise her"* returns and ends the poem with the same ring of praise.

**Note on the poem:** The refrain repeats, giving the poem a **musical, chant-like shape**. India is called "*she*", i.e. **personified** as a mother and a goddess, so the poem speaks to the land with love.

## ❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (with answers)

**1. "Mighty" is used for the Himavant — what does it express?**
→ It tells of its **grandeur and strength** — a high, huge mountain with no equal anywhere on earth.

**2. What is the effect of the question marks at the end of the fourth and sixth lines ("Which other river can match her grace?" / "What scriptures else to name with them?")?**
→ The questions are **rhetorical**. They are used to **emphasise** that no other river — no other scriptures — can stand comparison. They are not an expression of doubt.

**3. Why does the poet call the Ganga "generous"?**
→ The poet uses **personification**. As a mother gives freely to all, so the Ganga **gives water and life** to countless people living on her banks, without asking anything in return — that is her generosity.

**4. "The movement of the river is graceful" — what does "grace" suggest here?**
→ **Grace** here is the elegant, calm and beautiful movement of the slow-flowing river, like the dignified walk of an elegant lady (personification). No other river, says the poet, has such grace.

**5. Fill in the blank: the poet suggests that the Upanishads are (unmatched / abundant) in wisdom and spiritual depth.**
→ **unmatched** — "What scriptures else to name?" — there are none equal.

**6. "This sunny golden land" — what interpretation?**
→ India is **broad, bright, prosperous, fertile and beautiful** — gold representing wealth and glory, sunshine of hope and goodness.

**7. "She's peerless, let's praise her!" — what does that repeat do?**
→ It **repeats the central thought** of the poem, adds an **emotional, musical** touch, and stirs every listener to feel **pride and love** for the motherland — as a refrain of devotion.

**8. What does "many a sage has sanctified this land" tell us about India?**
→ Sages, through their penance and their teaching, have made the land **holy and pure**. So India is not only ancient — it carries the **blessing and purity of the enlightened**.

**9. How does the poem express the greatness of India's natural and spiritual beauty? (Model answer)**
→ The poet moves from **outer grandeur to inner knowledge**: the mighty Himalayas and the generous Ganga stand for nature's beauty; the sacred Upanishads, Brahma-knowledge and the Buddha's Dhamma stand for India's spiritual depth; and the gallant warriors, sages and divine music show its valour and culture. Through them he shows that India's greatness is written both in its land and in its spirit — and no country can match that.

**10. Is this an "ode"? Why?**
→ An ode is a **lyrical poem expressing deep praise for a person, a place, an object or an idea**. This poem is **to India**, to its beauty, its knowledge and its glory, in a language of love and reverence — so it is, indeed, an ode.
