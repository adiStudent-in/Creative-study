## 🧬 3. What the interview says — step by step

1. **The opening:** The interviewer greets "Namaste, ma'am!", calling it a privilege to meet a famous personality in the Paralympics. Dr. Malik replies she feels truly honoured.
2. **Named among the 10 most inspirational women para-athletes globally** by the International Paralympic Committee — asked how she braved the odds.
3. **The tragedy:** At **29**, she was diagnosed with a **spine (spinal) tumour**. After surgery, doctors declared she would be **bound to a wheelchair for the rest of her life** — **paralysed waist down**.
4. **The choice:** "I had two choices — **squander my life in remorse** or **transform it to a world of limitless possibilities**."
5. **Why para-athletics:** She **loved sports and had been a swimmer** → switched to **para-athletics**. Her Paralympics journey began.
6. **The breakthrough:** At the **2016 Rio Paralympic Games** she secured the **silver medal in the shot-put** — a moment of **personal victory and a step forward in changing perceptions**.
7. **Trailblazer:** She is the **first ever Indian female para-athlete to win an Asian Games medal in athletics** and **India's first ever female Paralympics medallist across any sport**. Her list of 'firsts' is never ending.
8. **Meaning of her awards:** "These achievements are a **testimony** to my belief that physical limitations **don't define one's potential**." She is a **proponent** of the school of thought **'ability beyond disability'**.
9. **The biggest challenge:** **Societal perceptions** — people often **underestimated her abilities**. She needed not only **physical strength** but also **mental resilience**; she **embraced challenges as opportunities**, and family support coupled with her **fortitude** let her **turn obstacles into stepping stones**.
10. **How sports break stereotypes:** When people witness the **strength, skill and competitive spirit** of para-athletes, it **breaks down preconceived notions**. Paralympics gave her **a new lease of life**, helped her **push boundaries**, and made her realise individuals with disabilities **can be as capable, if not more than, their able-bodied counterparts**.
11. **Her advocacy:** Strengthen the **emotional health of persons with challenges**; **empower women through outdoor sports and adventure activities**; support persons from **lower socio-economic strata**; provide **equipment to para sportspersons**; organise **disability sports awareness and advocacy sessions at schools and colleges** (youth is the voice of tomorrow). Goal: a society where everyone, regardless of ability, is treated with **dignity** and given a **fair chance to succeed**.
12. **Her advice:** "Every setback is an opportunity to prove your strength. Surround yourself with a **support system**, **believe in yourself**, and let your journey inspire others. Remember, **disability is not a limitation; it's a unique strength waiting to be unleashed**."

## ⚖️ 7. Fact–Opinion & Cause–Effect (Exercise II, III)

> **Fact:** evidence / factual information that can be proved to be true.
> **Opinion:** the writer's (or speaker's) beliefs / perception.
> **Cause:** actions / events · **Effect:** outcomes.

**Exercise II — answers:**

*(The two extra functions not used: (i) promise, (ii) possibility.)*

**Exercise IV — table of modal functions:**
| Modal | Functions |
|---|---|
| **could** | (i) possibility · (ii) unreal ability · (iii) request · (iv) suggestion |
| **couldn't** | (i) inability |

## 🌍 12. Extras from the Chapter (Good for MCQs)

- **Paralympics origin:** traced back to **1948**, when **Sir Ludwig Guttmann** organised a sports competition for **war veterans with spinal cord-related injuries** in **England**. The first Olympic-style games for differently-abled athletes (the **Stoke Mandeville Games**) were organised for the first time in **Rome in 1960**.
- The Paralympics are split into **Winter and Summer Games**, alternating **every two years**. Since the late twentieth century they have been held in the same city that hosts the corresponding Olympics, following shortly after.
- **Bharat's debut in the Paralympics:** **1968**; first medal clinched in **swimming in 1972**.
- **Olympics facts (infographic):** began as part of an ancient Greek festival celebrating **Zeus**; held every four years, alternating summer and winter; motto **'Citius-Altius-Fortius-Communiter'** = "Faster-Higher-Stronger-Together"; first Olympic Game took place in **776 BC**; the torch symbolises **peace and friendship** and is lit at **Olympia, Greece**; the Olympic rings (produced in **1913**) represent the union of the **five continents**.
- **Paralympic symbol (Agitos):** from Latin meaning **"I move"** — visual representation of the Paralympic Movement.
- **Special Olympics:** provides sports training and athletic competition for **children and adults with intellectual disabilities**; fosters the acceptance and inclusion of all people.
- **Special Olympic Games 2023 (Berlin, Germany):** **Ravimathi Arumugam** — gold, 400 m, Level C women's race; **Aanchal Goyal** — gold, 400 m, Level B women's race; **Saket Kundu** — rare double: silver (Level B mini javelin) + bronze (Level B 400 m men's race).

## 📜 2. The Poem

> **Stanza 1**
> The athletes had come from all over the country
> To run for the gold, for the silver, and bronze
> Many weeks and months of training
> All coming down to these games.
>
> **Stanza 2**
> The spectators gathered around the old field
> To cheer on all the young women and men
> The final event of the day was approaching
> Excitement was high to begin.
>
> **Stanza 3**
> The blocks were all lined up for those who would use them
> The hundred-yard dash and the race to be run
> These were nine resolved athletes in the back of the starting line
> Poised for the sound of the gun.
>
> **Stanza 4**
> The signal was given, the pistol exploded
> And so did the runners all charging ahead
> But the smallest among them, he stumbled and staggered
> And fell to the asphalt instead.
>
> **Stanza 5**
> He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish
> His dreams and his efforts dashed in the dirt
> But as sure as I'm standing here telling this story
> The same goes for what next occurred.
>
> **Stanza 6**
> The eight other runners pulled up on their heels
> The ones who had trained for so long to compete
> One by one they all turned round and went back to help him
> And brought the young boy to his feet.
>
> **Stanza 7**
> Then all the nine runners joined hands and continued
> The hundred-yard dash now reduced to a walk
> And a banner above that said (Special Olympics)
> Could not have been more on the mark.
>
> **Stanza 8**
> That's how the race ended, with nine gold medals
> They came to the finish line holding hands still
> And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces
> Said more than these words ever will.

## 📖 5. Appreciating the Poem (exercise answers)

**1. Alliteration (two examples):** e.g. *"the smallest among them, he **st**umbled and **st**aggered"* · *"**s**ilver and **s**ound of the **g**un"* (also *"poised for the sound of the gun"*).
> (Any two sound-repetition examples from the poem are accepted.)

**2. Visual imagery (three examples):** *"the pistol exploded"* · *"fell to the asphalt"* · *"the eight other runners pulled up on their heels"* · *"joined hands and continued"* · *"nine beaming faces"* · "a banner above that said (Special Olympics)" (choose any three).

**3. Auditory imagery (one example):** *"the pistol exploded"* · *"He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish"* · the spectators' cheers.

**4. How does imagery make the poem more appealing?** Imagery lets the reader **see and hear** the race unfold — the pistol, the fall, the helping hands, the upturned faces. It makes the events **vivid and realistic**, so we feel the excitement, the boy's pain, and the final joy as if we were there.

**5. How does the poet's tone change?** **Beginning** — excited, competitive (spectators, poised athletes). **Middle** — tense and sorrowful (the fall, the cry of anguish). **End** — warm, joyful, admiring (joined hands, standing ovation, beaming faces).

**6. Overall mood:** **Inspiring, uplifting and heart-warming.**

**7. Message of the poem:** In the spirit of the Special Olympics, **helping others and completing the race together is more important than winning**. Compassion and unity are the real victories.

## ❓ 6. Critical Reflection (Extract answers)

**Extract 1:** *The eight other runners pulled up on their heels / The ones who had trained for so long to compete / One by one they all turned round and went back to help him / And brought the young boy to his feet.*

- **(i)** "Pulled up on their heels" means the runners **→ B. stopped running**.
- **(ii)** One character trait common to all eight: **compassion / empathy / kindness** (or helping nature, selflessness).
- **(iii)** Tone of the poet: **warm, appreciative, admiring**.
- **(iv)** The young athlete probably felt **grateful, relieved, touched and comforted** — his defeat turned into belonging.
- **(v)** Yes — it is the **turning point**: a competitive race becomes an act of compassion, and leads to all nine winning gold medals.

**Extract 2:** *That's how the race ended, with nine gold medals / They came to the finish line holding hands still / And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces / Said more than these words ever will.*

- **(i)** The nine contestants felt **joyful, proud and united** (happy in each other's company).
- **(ii)** All nine were given gold medals because they showed the **true Special Olympics spirit** — they helped the fallen boy and finished **together, hand in hand**; each one was equally a winner.
- **(iii)** The holding of hands signifies a feeling of **unity / togetherness / friendship**.
- **(iv)** The 'standing ovation' indicates the spectators were **→ A. amazed**.
- **(v)** The last line means the audience's love and the athletes' joy were so powerful that **no words could express them fully** — the moment spoke for itself.
