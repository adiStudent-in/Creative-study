👤 2. About Dr. Deepa Malik (general knowledge — not in the textbook)

Dr. Deepa Malik is an Indian para-athlete. She is a Khel Ratna, Arjuna and Padma Shri Awardee, and was honoured with the Arjuna Award in January 2024 (seen in the opening picture of the chapter). She was listed as one of the 10 most inspirational women para-athletes globally by the International Paralympic Committee. (Note: the interview itself does not list her medals; the picture caption mentions the Arjuna Award.)

🧬 3. What the interview says — step by step

  1. The opening: The interviewer greets "Namaste, ma'am!", calling it a privilege to meet a famous personality in the Paralympics. Dr. Malik replies she feels truly honoured.
  2. Named among the 10 most inspirational women para-athletes globally by the International Paralympic Committee — asked how she braved the odds.
  3. The tragedy: At 29, she was diagnosed with a spine (spinal) tumour. After surgery, doctors declared she would be bound to a wheelchair for the rest of her life — paralysed waist down.
  4. The choice: "I had two choices — squander my life in remorse or transform it to a world of limitless possibilities."
  5. Why para-athletics: She loved sports and had been a swimmer → switched to para-athletics. Her Paralympics journey began.
  6. The breakthrough: At the 2016 Rio Paralympic Games she secured the silver medal in the shot-put — a moment of personal victory and a step forward in changing perceptions.
  7. Trailblazer: She is the first ever Indian female para-athlete to win an Asian Games medal in athletics and India's first ever female Paralympics medallist across any sport. Her list of 'firsts' is never ending.
  8. Meaning of her awards: "These achievements are a testimony to my belief that physical limitations don't define one's potential." She is a proponent of the school of thought 'ability beyond disability'.
  9. The biggest challenge: Societal perceptions — people often underestimated her abilities. She needed not only physical strength but also mental resilience; she embraced challenges as opportunities, and family support coupled with her fortitude let her turn obstacles into stepping stones.
  10. How sports break stereotypes: When people witness the strength, skill and competitive spirit of para-athletes, it breaks down preconceived notions. Paralympics gave her a new lease of life, helped her push boundaries, and made her realise individuals with disabilities can be as capable, if not more than, their able-bodied counterparts.
  11. Her advocacy: Strengthen the emotional health of persons with challenges; empower women through outdoor sports and adventure activities; support persons from lower socio-economic strata; provide equipment to para sportspersons; organise disability sports awareness and advocacy sessions at schools and colleges (youth is the voice of tomorrow). Goal: a society where everyone, regardless of ability, is treated with dignity and given a fair chance to succeed.
  12. Her advice: "Every setback is an opportunity to prove your strength. Surround yourself with a support system, believe in yourself, and let your journey inspire others. Remember, disability is not a limitation; it's a unique strength waiting to be unleashed."

⚖️ 7. Fact–Opinion & Cause–Effect (Exercise II, III)

Fact: evidence / factual information that can be proved to be true. Opinion: the writer's (or speaker's) beliefs / perception. Cause: actions / events · Effect: outcomes.

🌍 12. Extras from the Chapter (Good for MCQs)

  • Paralympics origin: traced back to 1948, when Sir Ludwig Guttmann organised a sports competition for war veterans with spinal cord-related injuries in England. The first Olympic-style games for differently-abled athletes (the Stoke Mandeville Games) were organised for the first time in Rome in 1960.
  • The Paralympics are split into Winter and Summer Games, alternating every two years. Since the late twentieth century they have been held in the same city that hosts the corresponding Olympics, following shortly after.
  • Bharat's debut in the Paralympics: 1968; first medal clinched in swimming in 1972.
  • Olympics facts (infographic): began as part of an ancient Greek festival celebrating Zeus; held every four years, alternating summer and winter; motto 'Citius-Altius-Fortius-Communiter' = "Faster-Higher-Stronger-Together"; first Olympic Game took place in 776 BC; the torch symbolises peace and friendship and is lit at Olympia, Greece; the Olympic rings (produced in 1913) represent the union of the five continents.
  • Paralympic symbol (Agitos): from Latin meaning "I move" — visual representation of the Paralympic Movement.
  • Special Olympics: provides sports training and athletic competition for children and adults with intellectual disabilities; fosters the acceptance and inclusion of all people.
  • Special Olympic Games 2023 (Berlin, Germany): Ravimathi Arumugam — gold, 400 m, Level C women's race; Aanchal Goyal — gold, 400 m, Level B women's race; Saket Kundu — rare double: silver (Level B mini javelin) + bronze (Level B 400 m men's race).

📜 2. The Poem

Stanza 1 The athletes had come from all over the country To run for the gold, for the silver, and bronze Many weeks and months of training All coming down to these games.

Stanza 2 The spectators gathered around the old field To cheer on all the young women and men The final event of the day was approaching Excitement was high to begin.

Stanza 3 The blocks were all lined up for those who would use them The hundred-yard dash and the race to be run These were nine resolved athletes in the back of the starting line Poised for the sound of the gun.

Stanza 4 The signal was given, the pistol exploded And so did the runners all charging ahead But the smallest among them, he stumbled and staggered And fell to the asphalt instead.

Stanza 5 He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish His dreams and his efforts dashed in the dirt But as sure as I'm standing here telling this story The same goes for what next occurred.

Stanza 6 The eight other runners pulled up on their heels The ones who had trained for so long to compete One by one they all turned round and went back to help him And brought the young boy to his feet.

Stanza 7 Then all the nine runners joined hands and continued The hundred-yard dash now reduced to a walk And a banner above that said (Special Olympics) Could not have been more on the mark.

Stanza 8 That's how the race ended, with nine gold medals They came to the finish line holding hands still And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces Said more than these words ever will.

📖 5. Appreciating the Poem (exercise answers)

1. Alliteration (two examples): e.g. "the smallest among them, he stumbled and staggered" · "silver and sound of the gun" (also "poised for the sound of the gun").

(Any two sound-repetition examples from the poem are accepted.)

2. Visual imagery (three examples): "the pistol exploded" · "fell to the asphalt" · "the eight other runners pulled up on their heels" · "joined hands and continued" · "nine beaming faces" · "a banner above that said (Special Olympics)" (choose any three).

3. Auditory imagery (one example): "the pistol exploded" · "He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish" · the spectators' cheers.

4. How does imagery make the poem more appealing? Imagery lets the reader see and hear the race unfold — the pistol, the fall, the helping hands, the upturned faces. It makes the events vivid and realistic, so we feel the excitement, the boy's pain, and the final joy as if we were there.

5. How does the poet's tone change? Beginning — excited, competitive (spectators, poised athletes). Middle — tense and sorrowful (the fall, the cry of anguish). End — warm, joyful, admiring (joined hands, standing ovation, beaming faces).

6. Overall mood: Inspiring, uplifting and heart-warming.

7. Message of the poem: In the spirit of the Special Olympics, helping others and completing the race together is more important than winning. Compassion and unity are the real victories.

❓ 6. Critical Reflection (Extract answers)

Extract 1: The eight other runners pulled up on their heels / The ones who had trained for so long to compete / One by one they all turned round and went back to help him / And brought the young boy to his feet.

  • (i) "Pulled up on their heels" means the runners → B. stopped running.
  • (ii) One character trait common to all eight: compassion / empathy / kindness (or helping nature, selflessness).
  • (iii) Tone of the poet: warm, appreciative, admiring.
  • (iv) The young athlete probably felt grateful, relieved, touched and comforted — his defeat turned into belonging.
  • (v) Yes — it is the turning point: a competitive race becomes an act of compassion, and leads to all nine winning gold medals.

Extract 2: That's how the race ended, with nine gold medals / They came to the finish line holding hands still / And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces / Said more than these words ever will.

  • (i) The nine contestants felt joyful, proud and united (happy in each other's company).
  • (ii) All nine were given gold medals because they showed the true Special Olympics spirit — they helped the fallen boy and finished together, hand in hand; each one was equally a winner.
  • (iii) The holding of hands signifies a feeling of unity / togetherness / friendship.
  • (iv) The 'standing ovation' indicates the spectators were → A. amazed.
  • (v) The last line means the audience's love and the athletes' joy were so powerful that no words could express them fully — the moment spoke for itself.

📝 LONG ANSWER (Complete summary — exam-ready)

Use this as a complete answer. Every important point of the chapter is here.

"The World of Limitless Possibilities" is an interview with Dr. Deepa Malik, a famous personality in the world of Paralympics. She was honoured with the Arjuna Award in January 2024 and is a Khel Ratna, Arjuna and Padma Shri Awardee. She has left an indelible (permanent) mark on innumerable aspiring athletes. The beginng of the Paralympics journey: Dr. Malik loved sports and had been a swimmer too, so she decided to switch to para-athletics. This is how her Paralympics journey began. Challenges and how she overcame them: One of the significant challenges for her was societal perceptions — people often underestimated her abilities. Overcoming this required not only physical strength but also mental resilience. She embraced challenges as opportunities to prove her capabilities. The support of her family, coupled with her fortitude (strength to face adversity), allowed her to turn obstacles into stepping stones.

How sports challenge stereotypes: Sports, especially Paralympics, have the extraordinary ability to challenge stereotypes and change attitudes towards disability. When people witness the strength, skill and competitive spirit of para-athletes, it breaks down preconceived notions (opinions formed before knowing the truth). Paralympics has given her a new lease of life and helped her push boundaries. Her goal is to contribute to creating a society where everyone, regardless of ability, is treated with dignity and given a fair chance to succeed.

Her advice: Every setback is an opportunity to prove your strength. Surround yourself with a support system, believe in yourself, and let your journey inspire others. Remember, disability is not a limitation; it's a unique strength waiting to be unleashed.

❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (with answers)

1. Explain how Dr. Malik's achievements challenge societal perceptions. Her achievements — winning a silver medal at Rio 2016, being India's first female Paralympics medallist, and being listed among the 10 most inspirational women para-athletes globally — prove that a person bound to a wheelchair can achieve what even able-bodied people find hard. 🟢 PART B — NINE GOLD MEDALS (POEM)

🎭 THEME

Nine Gold Medals by David Roth tells the inspiring story of a Special Olympics race. Nine athletes compete in a hundred-yard dash. The smallest and youngest runner stumbles and falls. Instead of racing ahead, the other eight runners turn back and help him; all nine then join hands and walk together to the finishing line. The race ends with nine gold medals and a standing ovation. The theme is empathy, compassion, sportsmanship and helping others — human compassion is more precious than winning.

💡 CENTRAL IDEA

Victory is not always about winning — sometimes it is about kindness. In a race where one runner fell, the eight others forgot that they were competing and helped the fallen boy up. All nine finished the race together, hand in hand, and were given nine gold medals. The poem shows that caring for others and the spirit of togetherness is a bigger victory than any medal.

📜 STANZA-WISE EXPLANATION (correct & complete)

Stanza 1 — The athletes and their dream

The athletes had come from all over the country To run for the gold, for the silver, and bronze Many weeks and months of training All coming down to these games.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • Athletes came from all over the country to compete for the gold, silver and bronze medals.
  • They had put in many weeks and months of training — all their effort came down to these games.

Stanza 2 — The spectators and the final event

The spectators gathered around the old field To cheer on all the young women and men The final event of the day was approaching Excitement was high to begin.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • Spectators gathered around the old field to cheer the young men and women athletes.
  • The final event of the day — the race — was approaching and excitement was high.

Stanza 3 — The race is about to start

The blocks were all lined up for those who would use them The hundred-yard dash and the race to be run These were nine resolved athletes in the back of the starting line Poised for the sound of the gun.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The starting blocks were lined up; the race to be run was the hundred-yard dash.
  • These were nine determined (resolved) athletes at the starting line, poised (ready) for the sound of the gun.

Stanza 4 — The fall

The signal was given, the pistol exploded And so did the runners all charging ahead But the smallest among them, he stumbled and staggered And fell to the asphalt instead.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The pistol exploded as the signal; the runners charged ahead.
  • But the smallest runner stumbled (stepped awkwardly) and staggered and fell to the asphalt (the hard road surface).

Stanza 5 — The fallen boy's anguish

He gave out a cry of frustration and anguish His dreams and efforts dashed in the dirt But as sure as I'm standing here telling this story The same goes for what next occurred.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The fallen boy cried in frustration and anguish — his dreams and efforts seemed dashed (destroyed) in the dirt.
  • But what happened next was surprising and wonderful.

Stanza 6 — The runners stop to help

The eight other runners pulled up on their heels The ones who had trained for so long to compete One by one they all turned round and went back to help him And brought the young boy to his feet.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The eight other runners pulled up on their heels (stopped running).
  • One by one they turned round and went back to help him, and brought the young boy to his feet — forgetting that they were competing.

Stanza 7 — Walking together

Then all the nine runners joined hands and continued The hundred-yard dash now reduced to a walk And a banner above that said (Special Olympics) Could not have been more on the mark.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • All nine runners joined hands and continued, turning the hundred-yard dash into a walk.
  • The banner reading (Special Olympics) could not have been more fitting — this gesture represented the very ethos of the Special Olympics.

Stanza 8 — Nine gold medals

That's how the race ended, with nine gold medals They came to the finish line holding hands still And a standing ovation and nine beaming faces Said more than these words ever will.

Meaning/Explanation:

  • The race ended with nine gold medals — all the runners reached the finish line still holding hands.
  • The standing ovation and nine beaming faces conveyed the joy and honour of the moment said more than words ever could.

❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (with answers)

1. What is the effect of the phrase 'pulled up on their heels'? It means the runners stopped running — they suddenly halted, even though they had trained so long to compete. It shows that their compassion overcame their ambition to win.

2. Why do you think all the nine contestants were given gold medals? Because they all showed the true spirit of the Special Olympics. They did not abandon the fallen boy; they helped him up and finished the race hand in hand. Their act of compassion and togetherness made every one of them a winner, so all nine received gold medals.

3. What does the transformation of the hundred-yard dash to a walk symbolise? It symbolises that human compassion is more important than competition and winning. When the runners turned a race into a walk to help a fallen mate, they valued kindness and unity over speed and victory.

4. What is the message being conveyed by the poem? The poem conveys that in the Special Olympics (and in life), helping others and showing compassion is a greater victory than winning a medal. The spirit of sportsmanship is to uplift one another — together, everyone wins.

5. How does the poet's tone change from the beginning to the end of the poem? At the beginning the tone is excited and competitive (spectators gathering, runners poised). In the middle it becomes tense and sorrowful when the boy falls. At the end it becomes warm, joyous and admiring as the athletes finish together and receive a standing ovation.

6. What is the overall mood created by the poem? The overall mood is inspiring, uplifting and heart-warming — it fills the reader with admiration for the athletes' compassion and a sense of hope and humanity.

7. Why might the young athlete have felt on being helped by the others? He must have felt grateful, relieved and deeply touched. Instead of being left alone in defeat, he was helped by his competitors and got to finish the race with them — his sorrow turned into joy.

8. Would you consider the incident of helping as a turning point in the poem? Yes. The moment the eight runners turned back to help the fallen boy, a competitive race turned into an act of compassion. This single act changed the entire course of the poem — from a story of losing to a story of nine gold medals and togetherness.

9. The holding of hands signifies a feeling of — unity, friendship and togetherness.

10. The spectators giving a 'standing ovation' indicates that they were → A. amazed.

11. What does the last line "Said more than these words ever will" mean? The standing ovation and the nine beaming faces were more powerful and moving than any words could express. The athletes' act of compassion spoke louder than language.

12. What might be the poet's purpose of writing this poem? To celebrate and promote empathy, compassion and collective support in sports (and in life), and to show that the spirit of the Special Olympics is about inclusion and caring for one another, not about winning.
