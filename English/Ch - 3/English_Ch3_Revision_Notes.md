## 🧭 1. At a Glance

| Item | Detail |

## 📖 2. Summary (Long version)

### Origin and ancient use
- The word **pankha (punkha)** originates from **'pankh'**, meaning **feather of a bird**.
- **Pankha** is used for all fans; **pankhi** denotes a small **plumed fan** used in ancient India.
- Evidence of the **pankhi** can be found in **Buddhist wall paintings at Ajanta**, dating to the **2nd century CE**. Other representations appear in **embroidery, sculptures and carvings**.
- In ancient times pankhas were used in **temples to fan deities** and in **royal courts to fan kings**.
- Sizes varied from a tiny **two-inch** fan to large ones needing a person's **full arm strength**.

### Trade and regional varieties
- Over time, pankhas became **significant cultural goods** distributed through **trade routes**. They were considered **exotic and stylish**.
- Although there was **commonality in use** across India, each village and town developed **its own variety** with distinct materials or intricate designs.

**Regional pankhas:**

| Region | Type of fan | Material / special feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Rajasthan** | Appliqué hand fan | Pieces of fabric in different shapes sewn onto another cloth with ornamental needlework |
| Rajasthan | Zardozi hand fan | Glittering, ornate, encrusted **gold threadwork** |
| Rajasthan | Temple hand fans | Made by **engraving brass**, long handle |
| Rajasthan | Painted hand fan | Cardboard fan with images; usually **offered to gods** |
| **Gujarat** | Mirror-work hand fans | Elegant pure **cotton** fans embellished with mirror work |
| Gujarat | Beads hand fan | Covered with colourful beads, **silver handle** (Gujarat = centre for bead craft in India) |
| Gujarat (Kutch) | Hand-stitched **leather** fans | Decorated with thread and wool at the seams |
| Gujarat | Embroidered hand fans | Traditional mirror work and **cross-stitch** embroidery |
| **West Bengal** | Sola hand fans | Made from the milky-white spongy centre of **sola** (a water grass) |
| West Bengal | **Tal Patar Pankha** | Palm leaf fans, easy to carry, kept as an article of possession |
| **Uttar Pradesh** | **Phadh** hand fans | Adorned with pure gold, silver **zari**, silk and satin frills |
| **Odisha** | Large palm leaf fans | Recognised for antiquity and rarity |
| **Bihar** | Bamboo hand fans | Colourful and sturdy |

- **Tribes** also made their own versions: **grass and metal** embedded using **bamboo sticks**; **cane and palm leaves** used, with **silk and brass** reserved for antique pieces; **geometrical patterns**, and **white ink + red background** combinations.

### The present and the future
- In modern times pankhas are **restricted to decorative purposes** and have become **traditional craft items**.
- **Risk:** with technology and innovative creations, the beautiful culture of pankhas **runs the risk of slowly losing its presence** among Indians.
- **Change:** once made for **personal use**, this handicraft has transformed into a **commercial business**, now providing **some livelihood** to India's artisans.
- **Preservation:** the first step is to **celebrate pankhas** and appreciate the **culture, stories and artistry** they carry. This lets contemporary pankha makers **demonstrate their craft, regain popularity**, and get a **commercial platform** for a **sustainable livelihood**. Initiatives like **pankha-making workshops**, within and outside handicraft exhibitions, spread awareness about the beauty and importance of the craft.

## 📚 6. Critical Reflection — Answers

### Extract 1 (pankhas as cultural goods)
**(i)** "Pankhas were one of the most popular items of commerce" — **False.** The text says they became *"significant cultural goods"* distributed through trade routes; it does not say they were among the most popular items of commerce.
**(ii)** The word **'traditional'** is used because different villages and towns developed their own varieties of pankhas with distinct materials and designs — they are rooted in each region's tradition, not made uniformly.
**(iii)** "They were considered exotic and stylish" is an **opinion and not a fact** because it expresses how people *felt* about pankhas (a judgment of taste), which cannot be measured or proved like a fact.
**(iv)** One reason for **commonality in use**: pankhas served the same basic practical purpose everywhere — they were used in temples, royal courts and daily life across India — so their *use* was similar even while their designs differed.
**(v)** Correct assertion for the reason (pankhas made of indigenous materials, unique to region, elaborate designs): **A. Each kind of pankha could be distinguished from the other.**

### Extract 2 (technology and change)
**(i)** Negative impact of technological advancement: the beautiful culture of pankhas **runs the risk of slowly losing its presence** among Indians (traditional fans are pushed aside by modern electric fans and innovation).
**(ii)** Pankhas are called a **'culture'** (not just an object) because they carry each region's traditions, stories, materials and artistry — a living heritage.
**(iii)** Line showing the changed role: *"Once made for personal use, over time this handicraft has transformed into a commercial business and now provides some form of livelihood to India's artisans."*
**(iv)** One benefit of increased demand: it **provides a sustainable livelihood** to artisans / gives them a commercial platform to sell their craft.
**(v)** Factor behind commercialisation: **B. economic demand.**

## ✍️ 7. Answer the Following — Model answers

1. **How does the title "Winds of Change" capture the essence of the chapter?** The pankha (fan) is an object connected to **air and wind**, and the chapter shows how the craft has **changed over time** — from ancient temple and royal use, to cultural trade goods, to a modern commercial business facing the risk of decline. The title joins the fan (wind) with the idea of change.

3. **Balance between preserving traditional craftsmanship and incorporating innovative designs:** Preserving the old techniques keeps the craft and its heritage alive; incorporating innovative designs keeps the pankha **relevant and attractive** to modern buyers. Together they prevent the craft from dying while giving it a sustainable future.

4. **How pankha-making workshops help preserve the craft:** They **spread awareness** about the beauty and importance of pankhas in Indian culture, let contemporary makers **demonstrate their craft** before people, help the craft **regain popularity**, and give artisans a **commercial platform** for a sustainable livelihood.

5. **Benefits of celebrating pankhas:** Celebrating the craft means appreciating the culture, stories and artistry behind it. This helps contemporary pankha makers **demonstrate their skill and regain popularity**, and provides them a **commercial platform** to create a **sustainable livelihood** — so the craft stays alive.

6. **How the restriction of pankha use to decorative purposes reflects change:** Once an everyday object of personal use, the pankha is now used mainly as **decoration and as a traditional craft item**. This reflects how modern life has replaced it with electric fans, and how its **cultural role** has shifted from utility to **heritage and beauty** — a symbol of tradition rather than a daily necessity.

# 🟢 PART B — CANVAS OF SOIL (POEM)

## 🏁 At a Glance

| Item | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Poet** | **Maya Anthony** |
| **Type** | Lyrical poem / allegory |
| **Rhyme scheme** | **AABB** (couplets: deep–seep, true–hue, sight–light, blue–new, wide–coincide, till–still) |
| **Key metaphor** | A **garden is a painting** — the soil is the canvas/palette, seeds are brushstrokes |
| **Central idea** | Gardening is a form of art; in the hands of gardeners, gardens become paintings |

---

## 📜 The Poem

> Palette of earth, rich and deep,
> Where dreams of gardeners seep.
> Brushstrokes of seeds, planted true,
> Awaiting spring's vibrant hue.
>
> Blossoms bloom, a painted sight,
> Dancing in the morning light.
> Shades of green, red, and blue,
> Nature's artwork, ever new.
>
> Each plot, a canvas wide,
> Where art and life coincide.
> In the hands of those who till,
> Gardens become paintings still.

---

## 📖 3. Stanza-wise Summary

**Stanza 1 (Earth = palette):** The earth is like a painter's **palette** — rich and deep. The dreams of gardeners sink ("seep") into it. Seeds are like **brushstrokes**, planted truly/straight, waiting for **spring's vibrant hue** (bright colour).

**Stanza 2 (Garden = painting):** Blossoms bloom into a **painted sight**, dancing in the morning light. Shades of **green, red and blue** make it **nature's artwork**, ever fresh and new.

**Stanza 3 (Plot = canvas):** Each garden plot is like a **wide canvas** where **art and life meet** ("coincide"). In the hands of those who till the soil (gardeners), **gardens become paintings** — still, permanent works of art.

## ❓ 6. Critical Reflection — Answers

### Extract 1: "Brushstrokes of seeds, planted true, / Awaiting spring's vibrant hue."
**(i)** The option that uses a metaphor: **B. She has a heart of gold.** *(A, C, D use other devices — personification/simile-like phrasing — not a direct metaphor.)*
**(ii)** "Planted true" implies that the seeds are planted **correctly, straightly, with care and hope** — faithfully, so they will grow.
**(iii)** The poet uses **'hue'** instead of 'colours' because a hue is a **shade of a colour**, a more precise and painterly word — it fits the poem's painting metaphor and sounds more artistic.
**(iv)** Summer : hot :: Spring : **vibrant** (colourful).
**(v)** Assertion (A) "Gardeners wait for Spring" is **true**; Reason (R) "Gardens are worth painting in Spring" is also **true** — but (R) is **not** the correct explanation of (A) (gardeners wait for spring because that is when plants grow and bloom). **Answer: B.**

### Extract 2: "Each plot, a canvas wide, / Where art and life coincide."
**(i)** "Each plot" refers to **each garden bed / plot of land** being tilled.
**(ii)** Option that imitates the rhyme scheme (wide–coincide): **A. beautiful and clear / laughter and cheer** (clear–cheer rhyme, like wide–coincide).
**(iii)** The line that conveys gardening blends aesthetic beauty with natural growth: **"Where art and life coincide."**
**(iv)** The plot is likened to a canvas suggesting that **the garden is a surface on which nature creates art** — a place where beauty is deliberately created.
**(v)** The poet uses **'wide'** instead of 'long' because **wide** suggests openness, vastness and grandeur — like a broad painting — and it also rhymes with "coincide."

## ✍️ 7. Give Reasons for the Comparisons — Answers

1. A painter is compared to a gardener because **both create beauty — the painter with brush and canvas, the gardener with seeds and soil; both are artists of creation.**
2. A palette is like earth because **both hold the materials from which art is made** — the palette holds colours, the earth holds seeds.
3. Brushstrokes are like seeds because **both are placed carefully to grow into something beautiful** — strokes build a picture, seeds grow into plants.
4. A canvas is similar to a garden plot because **both are the surface/space where art takes form** — paint on canvas, plants on soil.

## ❓ 8. Answer the Following — Model answers

1. **How does the metaphor 'Brushstrokes of seeds' enhance understanding of gardening as an art form?** It makes planting seem **deliberate, artistic and creative** — just as a painter lays each stroke carefully, a gardener plants each seed with care and vision. The garden becomes a picture, and the gardener a painter.

2. **Poet's perspective on nature and creativity (from "Each plot, a canvas wide, / Where art and life coincide"):** The poet believes **nature and art are one** — creativity is not separate from life; it grows from it. A garden is living art, where beauty and growth happen together.

3. **Does the imagery paint a vivid picture?** Yes — words like *blossoms blooming, dancing in the morning light, shades of green/red/blue* create bright, clear visual images that bring the garden alive in the reader's mind.

4. **Would adding yellow have helped?** Yes — yellow would add **warmth, brightness and variety** to the palette of green, red and blue, making the imagery of the garden even richer and more colourful.

5. **"Gardens become paintings still" — the poet's view on timelessness:** Nature's beauty is **permanent and timeless**. A garden, once created, remains a work of art that endures — a "still" (motionless, lasting) painting that nature keeps renewing.

6. **Justify the title "Canvas of Soil":** The poem's central image is the **soil as the painter's canvas** — gardeners create living art on it. The title perfectly captures the poem's metaphor of gardening as painting on the canvas of the earth.
