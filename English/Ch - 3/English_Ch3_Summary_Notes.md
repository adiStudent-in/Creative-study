# 🟣 PART A — WINDS OF CHANGE

## 🎯 THEME

1. **Cultural identity through craft** — the pankha (hand fan) reflects the traditions, materials and artistry of every region of India.
2. **Change over time** — from ancient temple and royal use, to cultural trade goods, to a modern commercial business that now faces the risk of decline.
3. **Preservation** — celebrating the craft and spreading awareness can keep the pankha alive and give artisans a sustainable livelihood.

> ❤️ **Message:** *A craft is not just an object — it carries a culture, a story and an identity. To preserve it, we must celebrate it.*

---

## 📝 LONG ANSWER (Complete summary)

**"Winds of Change" tells the story of the Indian hand fan, the pankha, and its journey from ancient times to today.**

The word **pankha (punkha)** originates from the word **'pankh'**, which means **feather of a bird**. **Pankha** is used for all fans, while **pankhi** denotes a small plumed fan used in ancient India. Evidence of the pankhi can be seen in the **Buddhist wall paintings at Ajanta**, which date back to the **2nd century CE**, and other representations appear in embroidery, sculptures and carvings. In ancient times pankhas were used in **temples to fan deities** and in **royal courts to fan kings**. They varied from a tiny **two-inch** fan to large ones requiring a person's full arm strength.

Over time, pankhas became **significant cultural goods** distributed through **trade routes** and were considered **exotic and stylish**. Although there was commonality in their use across India, different villages and towns developed **their own varieties**, using materials like **bamboo, cane, palm leaf, silk, brass, leather and silver**, with decorative beads and stones, depending on geography, culture and traditional rituals.

Each region made its own distinct pankha. **Rajasthan** is famous for its **appliqué hand fan** (pieces of fabric sewn onto another cloth with ornamental needlework), its **zardozi hand fan** (glittering gold threadwork), its **temple hand fans** (engraved brass, long handle) and its **painted hand fan** (a cardboard fan offered to gods). **Gujarat** has elegant **mirror-work cotton fans**, the **beads hand fan** with a silver handle (Gujarat is the centre of bead craft in India), Kutch's **hand-stitched leather fans** decorated with thread and wool, and embroidered fans with cross-stitch. **Bengal** makes delicate **sola fans** from the milky-white spongy centre of the sola (a water grass) and the **Tal Patar Pankha** (palm leaf), easy to carry and always kept in Bengali households. **Uttar Pradesh** has the **Phadh hand fan** with pure gold, silver zari, silk and satin frills. **Odisha** has large palm leaf fans, and **Bihar** has colourful, sturdy **bamboo hand fans** — both recognised for antiquity and rarity. Many **tribes** also make their own versions, embedding grass and metal with bamboo sticks, using cane and palm leaves, and reserving silk and brass for antique pieces, often with geometrical patterns in white ink on a red background.

In modern times, the use of pankhas has been **restricted to decorative purposes**, and they have become traditional craft items. With the advent of technology and innovative creations, the beautiful culture of pankhas **runs the risk of slowly losing its presence** among Indians. Once made for personal use, the handicraft has **transformed into a commercial business** and now provides some form of **livelihood** to India's artisans. The slight increase in popularity is factored by the different versions of the pankha being crafted.

The **first step to preserve** the craft is to **celebrate pankhas** and appreciate the culture, stories and artistry they carry. This allows contemporary pankha makers to **demonstrate their craft and regain popularity**, and gives them a **commercial platform** for a **sustainable livelihood**. Initiatives such as **pankha-making workshops**, within and outside handicraft exhibitions, help spread awareness about the beauty and importance of this craft in India's culture.

## ❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (Competency-based) — with answers

1. **How does the title "Winds of Change" capture the essence of the chapter?** The pankha is connected to air and wind, and the chapter shows how the craft has **changed** over time — from temple and royal use, to trade goods, to a modern commercial craft facing decline. The title unites the fan (wind) with change.

2. **"The structure and design of pankhas are testimony to the cultural identity of the region." Support with two examples.** (i) **Rajasthan's zardozi fans** reflect its rich tradition of ornate gold embroidery; (ii) **Gujarat's beads hand fan** reflects Gujarat's position as the bead craft centre of India. *(Also: Bengal's sola fans, tribal geometric fans.)*

3. **Evaluate how the balance between preserving traditional craftsmanship and incorporating innovative designs helps.** Preserving old techniques keeps the heritage alive; innovative designs make the pankha relevant and attractive to modern buyers. Together they protect the craft from dying while giving it a future.

4. **How might pankha-making workshops contribute to preservation?** They spread awareness, let contemporary makers demonstrate the craft, help it regain popularity, and give artisans a commercial platform for a sustainable livelihood.

5. **Assess how celebrating pankhas could benefit artisans and the craft.** Celebration appreciates the culture, stories and artistry behind the craft; it helps artisans demonstrate skill, regain popularity, and earn a sustainable livelihood — keeping the tradition alive.

6. **How does the restriction of pankha use to decorative purposes reflect the changing cultural role?** Once an everyday object of personal use, the pankha is now mainly decorative — a symbol of tradition and heritage rather than a daily necessity, because electric fans have replaced it in daily life.

7. **Why is the pankha called a "culture" and not just an object?** Because it carries each region's traditions, stories, materials and artistry — it is a living heritage, not a mere tool.

8. **What is the one negative impact of technological advancement?** The beautiful culture of pankhas runs the risk of slowly losing its presence among Indians, as modern innovation pushes the traditional craft aside.

9. **What is the meaning of "Winds of Change" in the modern context?** It shows that with time, technology and innovation, the craft's role has changed — yet it can be preserved if we celebrate and support it.

10. **What is the central message of the chapter?** Crafts carry culture and identity; to preserve them we must appreciate, celebrate and support the artisans who keep them alive.

# 🟢 PART B — CANVAS OF SOIL (POEM)

## 🎭 THEME

**Gardening is an art.** The poem compares a garden to a painting — the soil is the palette, seeds are brushstrokes, and the garden plot is a canvas. In the hands of those who till the soil, **gardens become paintings**. The theme celebrates nature's beauty and the creative power of gardeners.

---

## 💡 CENTRAL IDEA

**A garden is a living work of art.** Just as a painter creates on a canvas with colours, a gardener creates on the soil with seeds. Nature provides the palette of green, red and blue, and the gardener's skill turns a plot of earth into a beautiful, ever-new painting.

---

## 📜 STANZA-WISE EXPLANATION (correct & complete)

### Stanza 1 — The soil is the palette

> Palette of earth, rich and deep,
> Where dreams of gardeners seep.
> Brushstrokes of seeds, planted true,
> Awaiting spring's vibrant hue.

**Meaning:**
- The earth is called a painter's **palette** — rich and deep in colour and quality.
- The **dreams of gardeners** "seep" into the soil — gardeners pour their hopes and love into the land.
- Seeds are like **brushstrokes**, planted **true** (straight, carefully, faithfully).
- These seed-brushstrokes **await spring's vibrant hue** — the bright colours of spring flowers.
- **Poetic devices:** metaphor (earth = palette, seeds = brushstrokes), rhyme (deep–seep, true–hue = AABB).

### Stanza 2 — The garden is a painting

> Blossoms bloom, a painted sight,
> Dancing in the morning light.
> Shades of green, red, and blue,
> Nature's artwork, ever new.

**Meaning:**
- Flowers blossom into a **painted sight** — a picture come alive.
- They seem to **dance in the morning light** (personification).
- The colours — shades of **green, red and blue** — make it **nature's artwork**, always fresh and new.
- **Poetic devices:** personification (blossoms "dancing"), alliteration ("Blossoms bloom"), imagery, rhyme (sight–light, blue–new).

### Stanza 3 — The plot is a canvas

> Each plot, a canvas wide,
> Where art and life coincide.
> In the hands of those who till,
> Gardens become paintings still.

**Meaning:**
- Each garden plot is like a **wide canvas**.
- On it, **art and life meet** — beauty (art) and growth (life) exist together.
- In the hands of gardeners ("those who till"), **gardens become paintings** — lasting, still works of art.
- **Poetic devices:** metaphor (plot = canvas), rhyme (wide–coincide, till–still), alliteration ("Gardens become").

## ❓ IMPORTANT QUESTIONS (Competency-based) — with answers

1. **How does the metaphor "Brushstrokes of seeds" enhance the understanding of gardening as an art form?** It makes planting seem deliberate and creative — like a painter laying each stroke, a gardener plants each seed with care and vision. The garden becomes a picture and the gardener a painter.

2. **What can you infer from "Each plot, a canvas wide, / Where art and life coincide"?** The poet believes nature and creativity are united — a garden is living art where beauty and growth happen together. Art is not separate from life.

3. **Does the imagery paint a vivid picture?** Yes — "blossoms bloom, a painted sight / dancing in the morning light / shades of green, red and blue" creates bright, clear visual images that bring the garden to life.

4. **Would adding yellow have lent effectively to the imagery?** Yes — yellow would add warmth, brightness and variety to the green, red and blue, making the imagery of the garden richer and more colourful.

5. **What does "Gardens become paintings still" say about the timelessness of nature's beauty?** Nature's beauty is permanent and timeless — a garden, once created, remains a work of art that endures, like a "still" (lasting) painting.

6. **Justify the title "Canvas of Soil."** The poem's central image is the soil as the painter's canvas — gardeners create living art on it. The title perfectly captures the poem's metaphor of gardening as painting on the canvas of the earth.

7. **Why has the poet used 'hue' instead of 'colours'?** A hue is a shade of a colour — a more precise, painterly word that fits the poem's painting metaphor and sounds artistic.

8. **The poem is an allegory. Explain.** The garden symbolises larger ideas: **life's journey and growth** (the changing seasons and blooming flowers represent the cyclical nature of existence) and **harmony and diversity** (different colours together symbolise the beauty of diversity and harmony in the world).

9. **Why is the garden called "nature's artwork, ever new"?** Because every season the garden blooms again with fresh colours — nature keeps creating new art endlessly.

10. **What is the poet's view of gardeners?** Gardeners are **artists** — with their hands and love, they turn ordinary soil into living paintings, proving that creation is not only in art studios but in fields and gardens too.
